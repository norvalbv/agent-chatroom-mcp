\begin{abstract}
We study whether a hub-enforced multi-agent chatroom improves machine-checked task success over one
language-model agent or independent sampling with oracle-free selection at matched spend. The later
incomplete five-arm run did not adequately observe its pre-registered primary, short-thinking comparisons:
subscription accounts served different thinking regimes under the same model alias and settings.
Its long-thinking comparisons are exploratory, not a replacement confirmatory result.

The historical three-arm study covers two scope-capture programs from one failure family and one
numeric-formatting task, with 40 seeds per arm. A single agent passes 33/40, 28/40 and 14/40;
a three-seat chatroom passes 40/40, 40/40 and 6/40; independently sampled and selected attempts
pass 36/40, 39/40 and 7/40. The direct chatroom-versus-sampling tests do not distinguish the arms
on these tasks; this does not establish equivalence. Both aggregation methods fare poorly where
the shared wrong answer is the modal answer among independent attempts. These historical
comparisons retain their own collection windows and registered tests; the later run is a separate
study, not an additional replication of them. We report costs, account-associated regime changes,
and the limits of inference alongside the task results. The evidence does not establish that the
hub's consensus mechanics add accuracy beyond independent sampling, nor does it measure the value
of non-author review or brainstorming. Section~\ref{sec:study-status} identifies the current evidence
and the remaining gap in the primary comparison.
\end{abstract}
