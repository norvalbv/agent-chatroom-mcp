\begin{abstract}
We study whether a hub-enforced multi-agent chatroom -- blind independent opening answers, a
challenge-gated propose/amend/vote cycle, and an independently verified conclusion -- outperforms a
single language-model agent at matched budget, and whether any such advantage is distinguishable from
the advantage of simply sampling several independent attempts and aggregating them with no
deliberation at all. We evaluate three oracle-scored tasks with 40 paired seeds per arm: two programs
from one invented-language scope-capture family, and one exact-numeric-formatting task where a host
language's shortest-representation routine disagrees with the written specification at a single input.
A single Claude Sonnet agent (arm~A) passes 33/40, 28/40 and 14/40; a three-seat chatroom with the full
hub mechanics (arm~C) passes 40/40, 40/40 and 6/40; $k$ independent single-agent attempts submitted
through an oracle-free selector, matched to arm~C's realized spend ($k=10,8,7$; arm~K), passes 36/40,
39/40 and 7/40. On the family whose single-agent failures share one wrong answer that is not the
plurality, both structured deliberation and unstructured sampling-and-selection recover the correct
answer at a rate not statistically distinguishable from each other on either program (Fisher's exact
test, Holm-Bonferroni across a pre-registered family of six comparisons; a family-pooled, exploratory
comparison gives K~75/80 vs.\ C~80/80, $p=0.059$). The chatroom beats the single agent significantly on
each program individually (Fisher's exact test, $p=0.0117$ and $p=0.0002$) and on that family pooled
($p<0.001$); the sampling baseline beats the single agent significantly on one of the two programs (Holm
$p=0.009$) but not the other (Holm $p=1.000$). We report that asymmetry as a fact and do not treat it as
evidence that the chatroom and the sampling baseline differ from each other: the direct test of that
question, K vs.\ C, is not significant on either program, and inferring a K-vs-C difference from a
difference in each arm's own significance against A would be a fallacy. On the numeric-formatting task,
where the wrong behaviour is itself the population's modal answer, both aggregation methods do worse
than the single agent (K~7/40, C~6/40, A~14/40), against an oracle ceiling of 37/40; here the single
agent's numerical edge over the chatroom is not significant by Fisher's exact test ($p=0.069$), though
the pre-registered per-task rule in \texttt{paper/protocol.md}\,\S5 (a two-proportion $z$-test, since no
cell count is below five) gives $p=0.039$ for the identical counts -- a reader who holds strictly to that
literal rule would call it significant, and we report both rather than choose silently
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). We report cost per correct answer for every arm, including
the effect of large prompt-cache-read shares on billed cost, and two system-level cost measurements made
on this codebase's own build process (a token-consumption round and a peer-review round). The evidence
does not let us conclude that this hub's specific consensus mechanics are interchangeable with
independent sampling and oracle-free selection, nor that they clearly add something sampling does not;
we report what is and is not distinguishable at this sample size rather than round either way.
\end{abstract}
