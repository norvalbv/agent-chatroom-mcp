\begin{abstract}
We compare a single agent, a three-seat chatroom, and selection from independent attempts on machine-scored tasks at roughly matched mean spend. All results are dated measurements of \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} under the \texttt{sonnet} alias on recorded subscription accounts, not estimates for other models or deployments. In Study 1, the chatroom passed both interpreter tasks on every seed, against 61/80 single-agent passes across their shared task family. The vote arm passed 75/80; direct tests did not distinguish it from the chatroom. On a numeric-formatting task, one agent passed 14/40, the chatroom 6/40, and the vote 7/40. The lower chatroom rate was significant under the pre-registered test but not a later Fisher test. Voting selected the common wrong implementation even though correct attempts were often available. The vote prediction succeeded on one interpreter task but failed its stated significance clause on the other.

The same recorded alias and settings yielded sharply different thinking-token distributions across subscription accounts. Study 2 mostly ran on the long-thinking account, leaving its pre-registered short-thinking primary comparisons essentially unobserved. Its small, exploratory long-thinking comparisons distinguished no arm from one agent. These data do not identify the cause of the account difference or the marginal effect of any chatroom mechanic. The comparison shows why serving-account provenance belongs with each run's outcome.
\end{abstract}
