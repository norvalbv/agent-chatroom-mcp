\begin{abstract}
We compare a single agent, a three-seat chatroom, and selection from independent attempts on machine-scored tasks at roughly matched mean spend. All benchmark results are dated measurements on recorded subscription accounts, of \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} under the \texttt{sonnet} alias in Studies 1 and 2 and of \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} in Study 3, not estimates for other models or deployments. In Study 1, the chatroom passed both interpreter tasks on every seed, against 61/80 single-agent passes across their shared task family. The vote arm passed 75/80; direct tests did not distinguish it from the chatroom. On a numeric-formatting task, one agent passed 14/40, the chatroom 6/40, and the vote 7/40. The lower chatroom rate was significant under the pre-registered test but not a later Fisher test. Voting selected the common wrong implementation even though correct attempts were often available. The vote prediction succeeded on one interpreter task but failed its stated significance clause on the other.

The same recorded alias and settings yielded sharply different thinking-token distributions across subscription accounts. Study 2 mostly ran on the long-thinking account, leaving its pre-registered short-thinking primary comparisons essentially unobserved. Its small, exploratory long-thinking comparisons distinguished no arm from one agent. The comparison shows why serving-account provenance belongs with each run's outcome.

A post-hoc audit of 260 structured verification entries across 36 rooms found that most reported only builds, existing tests or a scripted smoke client, about a fifth an added probe or before/after check, and none a run of agents on a changed build. In the first auditor's coding of four rooms, re-runs were the largest review category and had the lowest defect-flagged share among categories with a stated checking method. A passing report of the author's own command satisfies the verification gate.

Study 3 gave pools of 20 independent items with hidden tests and a 30-minute deadline to one agent, to three agents each holding a fixed third, and to rooms of three and fifteen seats. In both completed pools the three-seat room landed 5 more items than the split, against the prediction that they would not differ. Splitting never landed more than one agent, and fifteen seats never more than three, while cost per passing item rose with the number of seats. The pools were close to the ceiling, and a third pool was stopped after two runs.
\end{abstract}
