\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When aggregation helps}
\label{sec:discussion-helps}

On the scope-capture family, the single-agent pass rates (33/40 = 0.825 and 28/40 = 0.70 in the RQ1
grid) mean the shared wrong answer is held by a \emph{minority} of attempts, not the plurality, on both
programs, though the two programs differ in how concentrated that minority is: on
\texttt{stamp-interpreter} all 7 failing runs share one byte-identical wrong answer, while on
\texttt{stamp-2} the 12 failing runs split into two distinct wrong answers (11 sharing one, 1 the other)
(\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}, "What the existing data already says"). Any procedure that aggregates
several independent attempts toward their plurality answer recovers the correct answer whenever the
correct answer is the plurality, which both single-agent pass rates satisfy with room to spare; the
exceptions are recorded rather than assumed away. Arm~K failed on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} in exactly
4 of 40 groups (seeds 113, 116, 120, 122), each with exactly 4 of 10 attempts correct; of these, 2
(seeds 113 and 122) are groups where the shared wrong answer was the outright plurality (6 of 10), and 2
(seeds 116 and 120) are 4-4 ties between the correct answer and one wrong answer, decided by the
pre-registered tie-break rule (lowest sub-seed) in the wrong attempt's favor -- not, as an earlier
account in \texttt{paper/amendments.md} stated, four plurality losses (a correction verified directly
against each group's \texttt{selection.votes} in \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k}). Both failure modes
are consequences of the same underlying fact -- the correct answer was not always a comfortable majority
of a given group's 10 attempts even though it is the population majority -- and both are properties of
plain plurality voting, not of anything the chatroom's specific mechanics would avoid or reproduce
differently. This is true whether the aggregation is
an explicit vote count (arm~K's plurality selector) or an emergent chatroom consensus reached through
blind openings, proposal and challenge (arm~C): both land at pass rates that are not statistically
distinguishable from each other on either \texttt{stamp} task (Section~\ref{sec:results}), despite
arm~C additionally paying for mechanics -- a challenge gate, an independently assigned verifier,
quote-checked votes -- that arm~K's non-communicating attempts never exercise at all.

\subsection{When aggregation hurts}
\label{sec:discussion-hurts}

On \texttt{bench-printf-format} the single-agent pass rate is 14/40 = 0.35 (a fresh, independent
280-attempt measurement puts the underlying rate closer to 0.311 -- 87/280; Section~\ref{sec:results}),
and the wrong behaviour -- incorrect \texttt{\%.17g} formatting of $1\times 10^{-7}$ -- is itself the
population's \emph{modal} answer: 26 of the 40 single-agent runs in the RQ1 grid share it. Both
aggregation methods therefore do worse than the single agent alone (K~7/40, C~6/40, versus A~14/40),
against an oracle ceiling of 37/40 (at least one of $k=7$ independent attempts passing).

The pre-registered calibration for arm~K's selector (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, printf calibration
entry) traces this precisely rather than leaving it as a bare number. The 40-attempt candidate pool
partitions into exactly two signature classes -- 14 passing candidates agreeing with each other on
every one of 9650 README-derived execution probes, and 26 failing candidates likewise agreeing with
each other on every probe, the two classes disagreeing only on the 24 probes that exercise the one
known trap. At $k=7$, a sampled group ties within whichever cluster is present and MBR-exec's winner is
simply whichever cluster is the local majority in that draw; because the true pass rate is 0.35, the
local majority is usually the wrong cluster (two independent closed forms bound the honest range without
simulation: hypergeometric, drawing 7 without replacement from the fixed 14-pass/26-fail pool, gives
0.1789; binomial, treating each draw as an independent 0.35 coin flip, gives 0.1998; the realized arm-K
rate, 7/40 = 0.175, sits just below both closed-form figures, close enough that the calibration's
mechanism -- not a distinct effect -- is the more likely explanation for the observed rate). The
three-seat chatroom fails on 34 of 40 runs; the
committed artifacts record the oracle's pass/fail verdict for each chatroom run but not which specific
wrong answer a failing run produced, so we cannot cite evidence that all 34 chatroom failures are the
same \texttt{\%.17g} case rather than a mix of independent misreadings. What the pass-rate alone still
supports is that arm~C's chatroom process does not correct the single-model bias behind
\texttt{bench-printf-format}'s failures any better than arm~K's selector does -- both land within one
failure of each other (6/40 and 7/40) -- not a claim about which specific wrong answer each failing run
produced.

\subsection{Does deliberation add anything beyond sampling?}
\label{sec:discussion-beyond-sampling}

This room's brief asked, before any arm-K number existed, whether the chatroom's specific mechanics --
blind openings against anchoring, a mandatory challenge before quorum, quote-verified votes, an
independently assigned verifier -- contribute anything a plain sampling-and-selection baseline at the
same cost does not. K vs.\ C is not significant on any of the three tasks under Fisher's exact test with
Holm-Bonferroni correction across the pre-registered family of six comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:results}); the family-pooled comparison on the two \texttt{stamp} tasks (K~75/80 vs.\
C~80/80) is exploratory, outside that pre-registered family, and itself falls short of the conventional
0.05 threshold ($p=0.059$). Taken alone, that would read as "no distinguishable difference between
deliberation and sampling on this family." The full, pre-registered set of comparisons complicates that
reading rather than confirming it.

\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}'s prediction~1 makes three separate claims, and the paper scores all
three as written rather than the two \texttt{paper/amendments.md} scored when the arm-K result first
came in: (i) point forecasts of about 0.99 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and about 0.90 on
\texttt{stamp-2}; (ii) K vs.\ C not significant on either task; (iii) K vs.\ A \emph{significant} on
both tasks. Claim~(ii) held on both tasks. Claim~(i) held, loosely, for \texttt{stamp-2} (forecast 0.90,
observed 39/40 = 0.975) but not for \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (forecast 0.99, observed 36/40 = 0.90),
for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:results}: the forecast resampled (with replacement) the 40
arm-A answers already collected in the RQ1 grid, and that grid's pass rate (0.825) itself overstated a
freshly measured, larger and independently drawn single-attempt rate (0.675; Section~\ref{sec:results}).
Claim~(iii) is where the two \texttt{stamp}
programs diverge: K beats A significantly on \texttt{stamp-2} (39/40 vs.\ 28/40, raw $p=0.0015$, Holm
$p=0.0090$), matching the prediction, but \emph{not} on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (36/40 vs.\ 33/40,
raw $p=0.518$, Holm $p=1.000$) -- while arm~C beats arm~A significantly on \emph{both}
\texttt{stamp} programs (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

We report that asymmetry as a fact and stop there, because concluding a difference between arm~K and
arm~C from a difference in whether each one's comparison against arm~A reaches significance would be
the difference-in-significance fallacy: "K vs.\ A is significant here and C vs.\ A is significant there"
is not a test of whether K and C differ from each other. The direct test of that question is K vs.\ C,
and it is not significant on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (Section~\ref{sec:results}). So the asymmetry in
claim~(iii) is a real, pre-registered pattern worth recording -- and \texttt{stamp-interpreter}'s own
single-agent rate was independently shown to be volatile across samples (0.50, 0.825, and 0.675 at
$n=10$, $n=40$, and $n=400$ respectively; Section~\ref{sec:results}), which is at least as plausible an
explanation for a comparison this close to the 0.05 line landing where it did -- but it does not, by
itself, license a claim that deliberation and sampling differ on this program. We record it because the
pre-registration asked for all three of prediction~1's clauses to be scored, not because it carries
evidential weight beyond the direct K-vs-C test already reported.

What the data support, stated at the size the evidence actually is: the chatroom beats the single agent
significantly on both \texttt{stamp} programs (family-pooled Fisher's exact test on the two programs
together, Holm-corrected $p<0.001$; Section~\ref{sec:results}), and the direct comparison between the
chatroom and the oracle-free sampling baseline (K vs.\ C) is not significant on either program, nor on
the family-pooled, exploratory comparison ($p=0.059$). If the chatroom's specific consensus mechanics
contribute something that independent sampling does not, this suite -- two programs in one
failure-mechanism family, one task in another -- is not powered to detect it, and a reader should not
extrapolate a general claim about deliberation, in either direction, from these three tasks alone.
