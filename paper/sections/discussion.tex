\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When aggregation helps}
\label{sec:discussion-helps}

On the scope-capture family, the single-agent pass rates (33/40 = 0.825 and 28/40 = 0.70 in the RQ1
grid) mean the shared wrong answer -- every failing single-agent run on each task fails identically,
on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} at one output token and on \texttt{stamp-2} at two -- is held by a
\emph{minority} of attempts, not the plurality. Any procedure that aggregates several independent
attempts toward their plurality answer recovers the correct answer whenever the correct answer is the
plurality, which is true of nearly every group of attempts at these observed rates. This is true whether
the aggregation is an explicit vote count (arm~K's plurality selector) or an emergent chatroom
consensus reached through blind openings, proposal and challenge (arm~C): both land at pass rates that
are not statistically distinguishable from each other on either \texttt{stamp} task
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), despite arm~C additionally paying for mechanics -- a challenge gate, an
independently assigned verifier, quote-checked votes -- that arm~K's non-communicating attempts never
exercise at all.

\subsection{When aggregation hurts}
\label{sec:discussion-hurts}

On \texttt{bench-printf-format} the single-agent pass rate is 14/40 = 0.35 (a fresh, independent
400-attempt-class measurement puts the underlying rate closer to 0.31; Section~\ref{sec:results}), and
the wrong behaviour -- incorrect \texttt{\%.17g} formatting of $1\times 10^{-7}$ -- is itself the
population's \emph{modal} answer: 26 of the 40 single-agent runs in the RQ1 grid share it. Both
aggregation methods therefore do worse than the single agent alone (K~7/40, C~6/40, versus A~14/40),
against an oracle ceiling of 37/40 (at least one of $k=7$ independent attempts passing).

The pre-registered calibration for arm~K's selector (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, printf calibration
entry) traces this precisely rather than leaving it as a bare number. The 40-attempt candidate pool
partitions into exactly two signature classes -- 14 passing candidates agreeing with each other on
every one of 9650 README-derived execution probes, and 26 failing candidates likewise agreeing with
each other on every probe, the two classes disagreeing only on the 24 probes that exercise the one
known trap. At $k=7$, a sampled group ties within whichever cluster is present and MBR-exec's winner is
simply whichever cluster is the local majority in that draw; because the true pass rate is 0.35, the
local majority is usually the wrong cluster (two independent closed forms bound the honest range without
simulation: hypergeometric, drawing 7 without replacement from the fixed 14-pass/26-fail pool, gives
0.1789; binomial, treating each draw as an independent 0.35 coin flip, gives 0.1998; the realized arm-K
rate, 7/40 = 0.175, is consistent with both). The three-seat chatroom's independent discovery of the
same wrong answer in 34 of 40 runs shows the same single-model bias operating inside deliberation --
three agents reading the same specification tend to make the same misreading of the same one clause --
not a distinct failure specific to the hub's mechanics.

\subsection{Does deliberation add anything beyond sampling?}
\label{sec:discussion-beyond-sampling}

This room's brief asked, before any arm-K number existed, whether the chatroom's specific mechanics --
blind openings against anchoring, a mandatory challenge before quorum, quote-verified votes, an
independently assigned verifier -- contribute anything a plain sampling-and-selection baseline at the
same cost does not. On this task suite we cannot show that they do. K vs.\ C is not significant on any
of the three tasks under Fisher's exact test with Holm-Bonferroni correction across the pre-registered
family of six comparisons (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the family-pooled comparison on the two
\texttt{stamp} tasks (K~75/80 vs.\ C~80/80) is exploratory, outside that pre-registered family, and
itself falls short of the conventional 0.05 threshold ($p=0.059$).

We are explicit that \emph{not significant is not the same claim as equal}. The pre-registration's own
point forecast for arm~K's \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate (0.99, from resampling the 40 pilot answers
by plurality) overshot the realized 36/40 = 0.90, because the 40-run pilot pass rate that forecast was
built on (0.825) itself overstated a freshly measured, larger-sample single-attempt rate (270/400 =
0.675; Section~\ref{sec:results}). A comparison built on 40 paired seeds, against single-agent estimates
that move this much between independently drawn samples of the identical harness and prompt, is
under-powered to rule out a real but moderate difference between arm~K and arm~C in either direction --
it is not evidence the two mechanisms are interchangeable, only evidence that this suite cannot yet
distinguish them.

What the data do support, stated at the size the evidence actually is: on the one family where the
chatroom clearly beats a single agent, an unstructured, oracle-free sampling baseline built at the same
price beats the single agent by a statistically indistinguishable amount. If the chatroom's specific
consensus mechanics contribute something that independent sampling does not, this suite -- two tasks in
one failure-mechanism family, one task in another -- is not powered to detect it, and a reader should
not extrapolate a general claim about deliberation, in either direction, from these three tasks alone.
