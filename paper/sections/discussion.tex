\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When aggregation helps}
\label{sec:discussion-helps}

On the scope-capture family, the single-agent pass rates (33/40 = 0.825 and 28/40 = 0.70 in the RQ1
grid) mean the shared wrong answer -- every failing single-agent run on each task fails identically,
on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} at one output token and on \texttt{stamp-2} at two -- is held by a
\emph{minority} of attempts, not the plurality. Any procedure that aggregates several independent
attempts toward their plurality answer recovers the correct answer whenever the correct answer is the
plurality, which is true of nearly every group of attempts at these observed rates. This is true whether
the aggregation is an explicit vote count (arm~K's plurality selector) or an emergent chatroom
consensus reached through blind openings, proposal and challenge (arm~C): both land at pass rates that
are not statistically distinguishable from each other on either \texttt{stamp} task
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), despite arm~C additionally paying for mechanics -- a challenge gate, an
independently assigned verifier, quote-checked votes -- that arm~K's non-communicating attempts never
exercise at all.

\subsection{When aggregation hurts}
\label{sec:discussion-hurts}

On \texttt{bench-printf-format} the single-agent pass rate is 14/40 = 0.35 (a fresh, independent
400-attempt-class measurement puts the underlying rate closer to 0.31; Section~\ref{sec:results}), and
the wrong behaviour -- incorrect \texttt{\%.17g} formatting of $1\times 10^{-7}$ -- is itself the
population's \emph{modal} answer: 26 of the 40 single-agent runs in the RQ1 grid share it. Both
aggregation methods therefore do worse than the single agent alone (K~7/40, C~6/40, versus A~14/40),
against an oracle ceiling of 37/40 (at least one of $k=7$ independent attempts passing).

The pre-registered calibration for arm~K's selector (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, printf calibration
entry) traces this precisely rather than leaving it as a bare number. The 40-attempt candidate pool
partitions into exactly two signature classes -- 14 passing candidates agreeing with each other on
every one of 9650 README-derived execution probes, and 26 failing candidates likewise agreeing with
each other on every probe, the two classes disagreeing only on the 24 probes that exercise the one
known trap. At $k=7$, a sampled group ties within whichever cluster is present and MBR-exec's winner is
simply whichever cluster is the local majority in that draw; because the true pass rate is 0.35, the
local majority is usually the wrong cluster (two independent closed forms bound the honest range without
simulation: hypergeometric, drawing 7 without replacement from the fixed 14-pass/26-fail pool, gives
0.1789; binomial, treating each draw as an independent 0.35 coin flip, gives 0.1998; the realized arm-K
rate, 7/40 = 0.175, is consistent with both). The three-seat chatroom's independent discovery of the
same wrong answer in 34 of 40 runs shows the same single-model bias operating inside deliberation --
three agents reading the same specification tend to make the same misreading of the same one clause --
not a distinct failure specific to the hub's mechanics.

\subsection{Does deliberation add anything beyond sampling?}
\label{sec:discussion-beyond-sampling}

This room's brief asked, before any arm-K number existed, whether the chatroom's specific mechanics --
blind openings against anchoring, a mandatory challenge before quorum, quote-verified votes, an
independently assigned verifier -- contribute anything a plain sampling-and-selection baseline at the
same cost does not. K vs.\ C is not significant on any of the three tasks under Fisher's exact test with
Holm-Bonferroni correction across the pre-registered family of six comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:results}); the family-pooled comparison on the two \texttt{stamp} tasks (K~75/80 vs.\
C~80/80) is exploratory, outside that pre-registered family, and itself falls short of the conventional
0.05 threshold ($p=0.059$). Taken alone, that would read as "no distinguishable difference between
deliberation and sampling on this family." The full, pre-registered set of comparisons complicates that
reading rather than confirming it.

\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}'s prediction~1 makes three separate claims, and the paper scores all
three as written rather than the two \texttt{paper/amendments.md} scored when the arm-K result first
came in: (i) point forecasts of about 0.99 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and about 0.90 on
\texttt{stamp-2}; (ii) K vs.\ C not significant on either task; (iii) K vs.\ A \emph{significant} on
both tasks. Claim~(ii) held on both tasks. Claim~(i) held, loosely, for \texttt{stamp-2} (forecast 0.90,
observed 39/40 = 0.975) but not for \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (forecast 0.99, observed 36/40 = 0.90),
for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:results}: the pilot pass rate the forecast resampled from
overstated the freshly measured single-attempt rate. Claim~(iii) is where the two \texttt{stamp}
programs diverge: K beats A significantly on \texttt{stamp-2} (39/40 vs.\ 28/40, raw $p=0.0015$, Holm
$p=0.0090$), matching the prediction, but \emph{not} on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (36/40 vs.\ 33/40,
raw $p=0.518$, Holm $p=1.000$) -- despite arm~C beating arm~A significantly on \emph{both}
\texttt{stamp} programs (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

That asymmetry is the most specific evidence in this paper for a difference between deliberation and
sampling, and we report it as such rather than average it away. On \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, the
structured chatroom shows a significant improvement over a single agent that the oracle-free sampling
baseline, run at the same cost, does not reach; on \texttt{stamp-2}, both aggregation methods reach
significance over the single agent, and neither reaches significance over the other. This is not
evidence that deliberation is generally superior to sampling on this family -- the two programs share
one failure mechanism and are one statistical unit throughout this paper precisely because of that
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), and \texttt{stamp-interpreter}'s own single-agent rate was independently
shown to be volatile across samples (0.50, 0.825, and 0.675 at $n=10$, $n=40$, and $n=400$ respectively;
Section~\ref{sec:results}), so a comparison this close to the border of significance is exactly where
that volatility would be expected to move a result across the 0.05 line by chance. We flag the pattern
because the pre-registration asked for it to be scored, not because the sample size supports treating
it as a settled mechanism.

What the data support, stated at the size the evidence actually is: on the family where the chatroom
clearly beats a single agent, an unstructured, oracle-free sampling baseline built at the same price
beats the single agent by an amount not statistically distinguishable from the chatroom's on either
program, and reaches conventional significance over the single agent on one of the two programs but not
the other -- the same split the chatroom itself does not show. If the chatroom's specific consensus
mechanics contribute something that independent sampling does not, this suite -- two programs in one
failure-mechanism family, one task in another -- is not powered to isolate it cleanly, and a reader
should not extrapolate a general claim about deliberation, in either direction, from these three tasks
alone.
