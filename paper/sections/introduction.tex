\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A chatroom can give several language-model agents a chance to challenge and revise one another's work. It also buys more attempts at the task. To assess what the conversation contributes, the relevant comparison is with independent attempts chosen without seeing the answer, at a similar cost. This paper makes that comparison on a small, deliberately difficult task suite, then shows how a change in the serving account limited a later, broader study.

The measured system is \texttt{agent-chatroom-mcp}. Every run reported here used \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} under the \texttt{sonnet} alias between 18 and 20 September 2026. The subscription account active at run time is reconstructed from a sanitized switch log. Pass rates, costs, and which task was near ceiling belong to those dates, model and accounts. They are not predictions for newer models. The study method and the need to retain serving provenance do carry forward; the observed minority-versus-majority pattern is a hypothesis to test elsewhere.

\textbf{Study 1} used seeds 101--140 on three fixed tasks, comparing one agent (A), a three-seat chatroom (C), and independent attempts with oracle-free selection (K). The two interpreter programs share one scoping trap and count as a single task family. The third task asks for exact numeric formatting. Arms A and C were interleaved by seed; K ran later that day, before the account switch logged at 16:38 UTC. Mean spend was matched between C and K, but individual paired seeds were not cost matched. All outcomes came from a machine oracle, not from the agents' own verdicts.

\textbf{Study 2} used seeds 501--520 and added a higher-effort single agent (AH) and a builder--reviewer pair (B). Its pre-registered primary comparison targeted short thinking. The run stopped with missing cells after most work was served by the long-thinking account. The resulting long-thinking comparisons were specified after data had been seen and remain exploratory.

The paper makes three narrow points. In Study 1, direct tests did not distinguish the chatroom from independent selection, even though both could outperform one agent on the interpreter family and both had lower observed success on printf. Across recorded accounts, thinking tokens differed under the same alias and settings, with one long-thinking single agent near ceiling on the measured interpreter task. Study 2 cannot answer its primary question: the intended short-thinking stratum was almost absent. These points are reported with the failed pre-registered vote clause, the test revision, costs, and the missing data, rather than folded into a claim that chatrooms or voting generally help or hurt.
