\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A chatroom can give several language-model agents a chance to challenge and revise one another's work. It also buys more attempts at the task. To assess what the conversation contributes, the relevant comparison is with independent attempts chosen without seeing the answer, at a similar cost. This paper makes that comparison on a small, deliberately difficult task suite, then shows how a change in the serving account limited a later, broader study.

The measured system is \texttt{agent-chatroom-mcp}. Studies 1 and 2 used \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} under the \texttt{sonnet} alias between 18 and 20 September 2026; Study 3 used \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} on 23 and 24 September 2026. The subscription account active at run time is reconstructed from a sanitized switch log. Pass rates, costs, and which task was near ceiling belong to those dates, model and accounts. They are not predictions for newer models. The study method and the need to retain serving provenance do carry forward; the observed minority-versus-majority pattern is a hypothesis to test elsewhere.

\textbf{Study 1} used seeds 101--140 on three fixed tasks, comparing one agent (A), a three-seat chatroom (C), and independent attempts with oracle-free selection (K). The third task asks for exact numeric formatting. All outcomes came from a machine oracle, not from the agents' own verdicts.

\textbf{Study 2} used seeds 501--520 and added a higher-effort single agent (AH) and a builder--reviewer pair (B). Its pre-registered primary comparison targeted short thinking. The run stopped with missing cells after most work was served by the long-thinking account. The resulting long-thinking comparisons were specified after data had been seen and remain exploratory.

\textbf{Study 3} asked a throughput question: with 20 independent, testable items from one repository and 30 minutes, does one agent, three agents splitting the items, a three-seat room or a fifteen-seat room land the most? Items and their hidden tests were written by an AI curator.

\textbf{A review audit}, added after Studies 1 and 2, examines what one seat's check of another seat's work consisted of, across 36 rooms run with a mix of models (Section~\ref{sec:results-review}).

The paper makes five narrow points. In Study 1, direct tests did not distinguish the chatroom from independent selection, even though both could outperform one agent on the interpreter family and both had lower observed success on printf. Across recorded accounts, thinking tokens differed under the same alias and settings, with one long-thinking single agent near ceiling on the measured interpreter task. Study 2 cannot answer its primary question: the intended short-thinking stratum was almost absent. Finally, a passing report of the author's own command satisfies the system's verification gate; most verification entries reported re-running existing checks, and none reported running agents on a changed build. In Study 3, a three-seat room landed more items than split work in both completed pools, while splitting did not beat one agent and fifteen seats did not beat three.
