\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Multi-agent large-language-model systems are routinely proposed as a way to improve on single-agent
accuracy, through debate, deliberation, or role-structured coordination. Whether the resulting gain,
where one is observed, is due to some property of the coordination protocol itself or is only the
familiar gain from sampling several independent attempts and aggregating them is disputed in the
literature, and at least one study finds that plain multi-agent debate does not reliably beat simpler
sampling baselines \citep{Smit2023} (see Section~\ref{sec:related-work}). Separating these two
explanations requires a budget-matched, oracle-scored comparison in which the sampling baseline is
built deliberately, not assumed away; that comparison is this paper's subject.

We report a controlled study on this project's own multi-agent coordination hub
(\texttt{agent-chatroom-mcp}), comparing three arms at matched spend on the same task briefs, scored by
the same hidden-fixture, machine-checked oracle in every case (no arm's own consensus process, and no
language model, is ever used to judge correctness -- Section~\ref{sec:method}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Arm A.} One agent, working alone, with a budget ceiling matched after the fact to arm~C's
  realized spend on the same task and seed.
\item \textbf{Arm C.} The hub's full mechanics -- blind independent opening answers, a challenge-gated
  propose/amend/vote cycle requiring supermajority quorum, and an independently assigned, machine-verified
  conclusion -- run by three agents.
\item \textbf{Arm K.} $k$ independent, non-communicating single-agent attempts on the same task and
  seed, one submitted by an oracle-free selector (plurality of normalized answers for exact-answer
  tasks; execution agreement across candidate programs for the code task), with $k$ chosen so arm~K's
  expected spend matches arm~C's.
\end{itemize}

Arms A and C are the pre-registered RQ1 comparison in \texttt{paper/protocol.md}; arm~K was added by a
later, independent pre-registration (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}) specifically to test whether any
RQ1 advantage the chatroom shows survives being compared against a cheaper, non-deliberative baseline
built to cost the same.

We evaluate on three tasks admitted, by a pre-registered discrimination rule (single-agent pass rate
0.3--0.7 over ten pilot seeds), from a pool of about 45 candidate task designs
(Section~\ref{sec:method}): two programs (\texttt{stamp-interpreter}, \texttt{stamp-2}) sharing one
invented-language specification whose one natural implementation strategy -- an environment chain --
contradicts a single stated scoping rule, and one program (\texttt{bench-printf-format}) requiring
exact numeric formatting against a specification that a host language's built-in shortest-representation
algorithm gets wrong at one specific input. Because the two \texttt{stamp} programs share this one
failure mechanism, the family, not the individual task, is this paper's unit of statistical analysis
throughout.

Our headline finding is not that the chatroom wins on the family where it does win -- it does, and by a
large margin over a single agent on both programs of that family -- but that, on the same family, an
oracle-free sampling baseline built at the same cost is not statistically distinguishable from the
chatroom on either program, and itself beats the single agent significantly on one of the two programs
but not the other. On the one task in the suite where the single-agent's own wrong answer happens to be
the population's modal answer rather than a minority misreading, both aggregation strategies do worse
than a single agent working alone. We report:

\begin{enumerate}
\item the head-to-head RQ1 result on all three tasks, with Fisher's exact test and Holm-Bonferroni
  correction, effect sizes, and raw and adjusted $p$-values reported side by side (Section~\ref{sec:results});
\item the mechanism behind the two opposite outcomes on these tasks -- when the population's shared
  wrong answer is the plurality, aggregation of either kind hurts; when it is not, aggregation of either
  kind helps (Section~\ref{sec:discussion});
\item cost per correct answer for every arm, with the prompt-cache-read accounting that dominates
  billed cost on Claude seats made explicit (Section~\ref{sec:results});
\item two system-level cost and process measurements made on this codebase's own build history -- a
  token-consumption round and a peer-review round -- reported as system contributions in their own
  right, not folded into the RQ1 numbers (Section~\ref{sec:system});
\item every retraction, forking path, and deviation from the pre-registration encountered along the
  way, reported rather than smoothed over (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
\end{enumerate}

This paper reports only RQ1 (and the arm-K extension to it) as run. RQ2 (the chatroom against a
no-chat builder-plus-reviewer pair) and RQ3 (per-mechanic ablation across the seven hub mechanics listed
in \texttt{paper/protocol.md}\,\S2.3) are both pre-registered but have no execution behind them in this
codebase, and are listed as future work rather than as negative results (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
