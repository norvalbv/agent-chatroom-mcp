\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

This paper answers RQ1 only: at matched budget, does a multi-agent chatroom room reach a higher
task-success rate than a single agent, and how does that comparison change against a third arm that
spends the same budget on $k$ independent, non-communicating single-agent attempts with an
oracle-free selection rule. RQ2 (a no-chat builder-plus-reviewer baseline) and RQ3 (per-mechanic
ablation) were pre-registered alongside RQ1 but were not run; they are carried into
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} as future work, not silently dropped.

\subsection{Task admission}
\label{sec:method:admission}

The task suite is not the three tasks named in the original pre-registration. A five-seed screen
followed by a ten-seed measurement on the single-agent arm was run against roughly forty-five
candidate task designs across several build rooms; the admission rule, fixed before any arm-C or
arm-K run, buckets a task by its single-agent pass rate on ten seeds: $0.3$--$0.7$ inclusive is
\emph{primary} and enters the comparison reported here; above $0.7$ up to $0.9$ is a \emph{weak
discriminator}, kept but excluded from the primary comparison; above $0$ and below $0.3$ is a
\emph{low discriminator}, likewise excluded; above $0.9$, or exactly $0$, is retired outright. A
three-seed screen may reject a task early (three passes out of three) without spending the full ten
seeds, but no task is \emph{admitted} on fewer than ten. This bar is stricter than an earlier version
of the same rule, which admitted on as few as one pass in five seeds and was found, on further
measurement, to have let through two tasks whose true single-agent rate was near $0.9$ --- a
difficulty level five seeds cannot distinguish from a coin flip in the other direction, and no
feasible seed count can turn into a fair test of team advantage.

Of roughly forty-five candidate designs, three tasks in two families survived: \texttt{stamp-interpreter}
and \texttt{stamp-2} (family \emph{scope-capture}: an invented, nested-scope interpreter language whose
specification does not give a procedure defined inside another call access to that call's own local
variables, against the natural implementation choice of a lexical environment chain (a closure) that
grants it access anyway), and \texttt{bench-printf-format} (family \emph{libc-exact-number}: reproducing
a host's floating-point formatting exactly, including one case, \texttt{\%.17g} of $10^{-7}$, where the
natural implementation choice --- the language runtime's own shortest round-trip representation ---
disagrees with the specification's stated exact-binary-value rule). On the ten-seed admission pilot that
set each task's status, every failure on every admitted task traced to a single shared item: all five
\texttt{stamp-interpreter} failures and all five \texttt{stamp-2} failures were each their own program's
identical wrong output, and every \texttt{bench-printf-format} failure was the identical failing case
(the confirmatory grid's own failure counts and their exact composition, which is not identical across
every failing seed on \texttt{stamp-2}, are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, not repeated here).
This makes each admitted task a \emph{single-trap} task in the sense that its admission-pilot rate
reflects the chance of one specific slip, not a smooth difficulty gradient, and it is why the paper
treats the two \texttt{stamp} programs as one family rather than as two independent draws: they share
the same specification and the same shared-error mechanism, so a team advantage on one is not
independent evidence of a team advantage on the other. A
fourth task originally proposed for the suite, a long, resource-constrained instruction-following brief
scored by survival rather than exact answer, was retired rather than built: its scoring mechanism is
inherently unavailable to a single, non-chatroom agent (there is no room, no board, and no
\texttt{leave\_room} reason for arm~A to produce), so no version of it can produce a signal comparable
across all three arms, which this suite's whole purpose requires.

Every admission record also states, per task, the non-author attack that was attempted before
admission --- a seat other than the task's author trying to determine the oracle answer from the
agent-visible files alone, without solving the task as specified --- and the specification clauses a
passing solution does not actually have to exercise, so that an admitted task's apparent determinacy is
not an artifact of an inert or ambiguous clause silently doing no work.

\subsection{Experimental conditions: arms A, C, and K}
\label{sec:method:arms}

All three arms run on the same \texttt{sonnet} model alias (recorded model id \texttt{claude-sonnet-5}),
the same verbatim task brief, and the same hidden-fixture, machine-checked oracle; no arm sees another
arm's transcript, and every run's hub-entry hash, task-fixture hash, and scorer hash are pinned and
recorded so that a later mismatch is flagged rather than silently scored. The alias is not a dated model
snapshot: Section~\ref{sec:method:window} reports a mid-study change in the model actually served under
it, discovered after these runs, which is why every cost and token figure in this paper is reported
alongside the date and regime it was measured in rather than treated as a stable per-model constant.

\paragraph{Arm A --- single agent, budget-matched.} One Claude Code seat, given the task brief and
ordinary file/shell tools, with no access to any chatroom tool. Because the \texttt{claude} CLI exposes
neither a token nor a turn ceiling, budget matching is operationalized as two concrete limits, both
derived after the fact from arm~C's realized spend on the identical (task, seed) pair: a dollar cap
equal to arm~C's realized cost, and a wall-clock cap equal to arm~C's realized duration. This is a
paired, within-task, within-seed match, not a single number reused across tasks; it is also a
sequencing dependency this design accepts rather than hides --- arm~A cannot be run to completion on a
given (task, seed) until arm~C has already completed on it.

\paragraph{Arm C --- chatroom, full hub.} A three-seat room running the complete mechanism set of
Section~\ref{sec:system:primitives}: blind openings, propose/amend, quote-checked votes, the challenge
gate, and \texttt{require\_verification} with hub-assigned review. Three seats is the smallest room
size at which the challenge gate binds while still leaving a reviewer distinct from both the proposer
and the sole challenger; as noted in Section~\ref{sec:system:primitives}, this also makes arm~C's own
supermajority quorum unanimous in practice at this seat count, not a partial supermajority.

\paragraph{Arm K --- $k$ independent attempts, oracle-free selection.} Arm~K spends approximately what
arm~C spends on the same (task, seed) by running $k$ unmodified, non-communicating arm-A attempts and
submitting exactly one of them, chosen by a rule that never sees the oracle. $k$ is fixed, per task,
before any arm-K run, as $\lfloor \text{mean arm-C cost} / \text{mean arm-A cost} \rfloor$ over the
already-completed forty-seed grid: $k=10$ for \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, $k=8$ for \texttt{stamp-2},
$k=7$ for \texttt{bench-printf-format}. Flooring guarantees the arm's expected spend does not exceed
arm~C's; the comparison is judged on realized spend, not the nominal $k$, and if realized arm-K spend
ever exceeded arm~C's on a task, that would be reported as a defect of the matching, not repaired after
the fact by discarding attempts. Every attempt runs under a per-attempt cap (a \$0.30 dollar cap and a
150-second wall-clock deadline) chosen with roughly three-fold headroom over the observed arm-A maximum,
so the cap is not expected to bind; an attempt killed by its cap is kept in the group as a null vote
(no answer, no signature) rather than dropped, and its cost is counted. Selection is oracle-free by
construction: for the two exact-answer tasks, the submitted answer is the plurality of the $k$ attempts'
normalized answers, ties broken toward the lowest sub-seed --- the self-consistency method of
\citet{selfconsistency2022}. For the code task, the primary selector is minimum Bayes risk over
execution (MBR-exec, \citet{mbrexec2022}): each attempt's implementation is run against a fixed,
$9{,}650$-case probe set generated deterministically from the task's own public specification (never
from the hidden oracle or fixtures), and the attempt whose output agrees with the most other attempts,
summed pairwise across probes, is selected; a secondary, exploratory selector reduces CodeT's dual
execution-agreement check \citep{codet2022} to agreement among candidates alone, since the probe set here
is fixed rather than model-generated. An attempt that fails to load, throws on import, or times out
during selection contributes no vote and is reported separately, as an \emph{unloadable candidate}, from
a \emph{null vote} (an attempt that produced nothing at all, because its own cap or deadline killed it):
the two are counted in separate columns because they call for different remedies, and a group in which
every attempt is either null or unloadable is scored as an arm-K failure, never excluded from the
denominator.

A calibration was run before any real arm-K spend, using the forty already-completed arm-A attempts on
\texttt{bench-printf-format} as a fixed resampling pool: drawing groups of seven without replacement and
applying MBR-exec shows the selector choosing a passing candidate at a rate of approximately $0.18$,
below the $0.30$--$0.40$ band that had been fixed in advance as indistinguishable from a disguised random
pick, and well below the single-attempt pass rate of $0.35$. This is not sampling noise: every passing
candidate in the pool agrees with every other passing candidate on all $9{,}650$ probes, every failing
candidate likewise agrees with every other failing candidate, and the two clusters disagree only on the
specific case the task's single trap turns on --- so a group of seven ties within whichever cluster is
locally more numerous, and MBR-exec's winner is simply that local majority. Because the correct cluster
is the population minority ($14$ of $40$), agreement-based selection is expected, and was predicted in
advance, to actively select the wrong cluster more often than not. The result of the calibration is
reported as a mechanism finding in Section~\ref{sec:results}, not as evidence that agreement-based
selection cannot work in general.

\subsection{Statistical procedure}
\label{sec:method:stats}

\texttt{paper/protocol.md} \S5, as written and pre-registered before any RQ1 run existed, selects the
arm~A vs.\ arm~C significance test per task: a two-proportion $z$-test, or Fisher's exact test only when
a cell of the $2\times2$ table is below five. This remains the currently pre-registered rule; it has not
been superseded. The primary arm~A vs.\ arm~C comparison this paper actually reports
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) deviates from it in two respects, and the deviation is disclosed rather than
silently substituted: it applies a two-sided Fisher exact test uniformly, computed directly rather than
via the $z$ approximation, regardless of cell count; and, for the two \texttt{stamp} tasks, it pools
\texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \texttt{stamp-2} into one interpreter-family $2\times2$ table before
testing, extending to the significance test a family-as-unit-of-analysis rule that this project's
amendment record establishes for task \emph{selection} (Section~\ref{sec:method:admission}), not
originally for hypothesis testing. Both figures are reported side by side in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, by test name, and neither is presented as superseding the other: a reader who
holds this paper to its written pre-registration should treat the per-task $z$-test as the pre-registered
result and the family-pooled Fisher result as an exploratory revision made after the data existed. The
practical stakes of this choice are concrete on \texttt{bench-printf-format} (a single-task family, so
family pooling does not apply to it and only the test-choice deviation is at work): the pre-registered
per-task $z$-test on the $14/40$ vs.\ $6/40$ table gives $p=0.039$ (nominally significant at
$\alpha=0.05$), while the Fisher exact test on the identical table gives $p=0.069$ (not significant).

For the arm-K comparisons specifically, the pre-registered family is fixed at six two-sided tests (three
tasks $\times$ \{K vs.\ C, K vs.\ A\}), corrected by Holm-Bonferroni; both the raw and the
Holm-adjusted $p$-value are reported side by side for every comparison in that family, and the family
size is held at six even where a comparison cannot be computed from the data available, so that a
missing or partial cell cannot silently shrink the correction. The oracle ceiling reported alongside
arm~K --- the fraction of groups in which \emph{any} of the $k$ attempts would have passed, had the
selector been omniscient --- is stated explicitly as a bound on what selection could achieve, never as
an arm, and is outside this statistical family.

\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}'s first prediction, written before any arm-K run existed, has three
separate clauses: a point forecast for each interpreter task's arm-K pass rate, a claim that arm~K vs.\
arm~C is not significant on both interpreter tasks, and a claim that arm~K vs.\ arm~A \emph{is}
significant on both. All three clauses are scored in this paper as written; a prediction that holds on
two of its three clauses is not reported as holding, and Section~\ref{sec:results} states explicitly
which clause failed and on which task.

\subsection{The window-control experiment}
\label{sec:method:window}

Arm~C's forty-seed record on the interpreter family and arm~K's forty-fresh-seed record were measured
in different, non-overlapping time windows (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A single Sonnet agent's own
pass rate on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} differed noticeably across three measurements in this project's
own data --- $0.50$ on the original ten-seed admission pilot, $0.825$ on the forty-seed grid, and
$0.675$ on the four hundred individual arm-A attempts underlying arm~K's forty groups. Both the
forty-seed grid and arm~K's fresh attempts, along with a separate sixty-run cap-value control run around
the same time, were completed before approximately 15:30 on the day of these runs, so this specific gap
sits entirely inside one time window and is not an artifact of the two measurements straddling different
conditions; it remains unexplained by anything identified in this project to date, and is reported as
such rather than attributed to a cause. A distinct, later regime change is relevant to this paper's
\emph{other} within-day comparisons: between approximately 15:30 and 16:45, with no change to the
harness, its flags, the Claude Code version, or the recorded model id, the model served under the
\texttt{sonnet} alias began producing substantially longer internal reasoning per turn --- a single-agent
\texttt{stamp-interpreter} attempt's output tokens rose roughly six-fold (about 1.6K to about 10K) and
its cost rose from about \$0.058 to \$0.18--\$0.26; arm~C seats showed the same shift (a three-seat room's
cost rising from about \$0.60 to \$1.50--\$2.21). Because a provider-side change of this kind cannot be
ruled out as a general risk to any comparison that spans time, no number in this paper pools runs from
before and after a detected regime boundary, and every reported cost figure states the date and the
observed output-token scale it was measured at. The window-control experiment is the direct test of
within-window validity for arm~C against arm~K specifically: seeds of both arms run interleaved, seed by
seed, on the same task, so that whatever the underlying single-agent rate or cost regime is doing on a
given day affects both arms' seeds contemporaneously rather than one arm's seeds systematically earlier
or later than the other's. Its original design (twenty seeds, 201--220, both interpreter tasks) was
interrupted after three runs when the new, higher-cost regime made the planned run count exceed its
budget; it was restarted at reduced scope --- \texttt{stamp-interpreter} only, seeds 201--212, entirely
within the new regime --- and is reported under that revised design, with only its complete result read
as a result; a partial group of runs from the interrupted first attempt is not cited as evidence either
way.
This experiment is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} if it completed by the time that section was
finalized, and reported as pending, with the design above, if it did not; no partial set of its result
files is read as a result at any point in this paper.
