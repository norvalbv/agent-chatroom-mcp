\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We situate the hub against five bodies of work. Every citation below was fetched directly
from \url{arxiv.org/abs/<id>} (never taken from memory or from a secondary summary); the
full claim-by-claim accounting, including which parts of this system each source makes
non-novel, lives in \texttt{paper/related-work.md} in the accompanying repository.

\subsection{Multi-agent debate and deliberation}

Multi-round debate among LLM instances improves factual accuracy and reasoning over
single-model sampling \citep{Du2023}; this is the base claim the hub builds on, not a
contribution of this paper. Plain debate is also known to fail in specific, documented
ways that motivate the hub's structural gates rather than leaving them as untested design
choices: agents converge and stop generating novel content without an enforced divergence
mechanism \citep{Liang2023}; debate does not reliably beat simpler baselines such as
self-consistency sampling-and-voting, and any benefit is protocol-dependent
\citep{Smit2023}; excessive agreeableness collapses debate into premature, incorrect
consensus faster than a single agent errs \citep{Yao2025}; debate suppresses the output
diversity that open-ended tasks need \citep{Nguyen2026}; and a correct minority position
can be outvoted with no mechanism to overturn it, though a learned meta-classifier can
sometimes detect and reverse such cases \citep{He2026}. This system's blind-opening
reveal, mandatory pre-quorum challenge, and quote-checked votes are a direct, but
unmeasured beyond this paper's own RQ1, response to the first four failure modes; its
supermajority quorum has no analogue of \citeauthor{He2026}'s override, so a correct
minority is simply outvoted once quorum is reached, a limitation stated plainly in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}. Per-agent reliability estimated from confidence signals can
reweight contributions and catch shared misconceptions that plain majority voting cannot
\citep{Jin2026}; the hub's nearest analogue is a \emph{structural} check (a named other
seat produces a machine-checked verification verdict) rather than a \emph{learned} one,
and we do not claim the structural version is better --- that comparison is untested here.

\subsection{LLM software-engineering agent teams}

Autonomous multi-agent cooperation on tasks without continuous human steering is
established prior art \citep{Li2023,Wu2023}: role-playing agents can cooperate via
inception prompting \citep{Li2023}, and general conversable multi-agent frameworks
combine agents, humans and tools \citep{Wu2023}. This system does not claim to have
invented multi-agent LLM collaboration; its structure is a specific hub protocol (quorum,
challenge, verify gate) layered on top of free-form conversation, narrower than a
general-purpose framework. The sharpest threat to any claim that this hub's benefit comes
from its protocol rather than merely from sampling more agent instances is the finding
that sampling-and-voting across more agent instances improves performance roughly in
proportion to agent count, independent of other techniques \citep{Li2024}. Arm K in this
paper's own RQ1 (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is designed specifically to separate ``more
agents helped'' from ``the hub mechanics helped,'' using \citeauthor{Li2024}'s premise as
the null it must be tested against rather than an assumption we can grant for free.

\subsection{Verification, self-report reliability, and group-decision stability}

The hub's electorate and quorum design draws on coalition-formation game theory rather
than LLM-verification literature, and we are explicit that these are a different
literature than ``can an LLM's self-report be trusted.'' Emotional dynamics can guarantee
convergence to stable coalition outcomes under conditions where classical models do not
converge \citep{Boehmer2022}; stability concepts have been analyzed for hedonic games
with sequential, immediate-assignment arrival \citep{Bullinger2023}; and an anytime
algorithm can bound coalition-structure quality relative to optimal, tightening the bound
with more computation \citep{Sandholm1998}. These are cited only as background for the
idea that a group admitting participants over time needs a stability argument, not
because the hub implements any of their specific mechanisms --- the hub's supermajority
quorum is a fixed heuristic, not a worst-case-guaranteed algorithm, and stating this
precisely matters because a citation of \citeauthor{Sandholm1998} could otherwise be
misread as claiming a formal guarantee the code does not provide. The question of whether
an agent's own claim to have verified something can be trusted is answered here by the
hub's own mechanism (a \texttt{verify/*} entry counts only if it parses as JSON with
\texttt{exit\_code:0} and the exact proposal id, from a seat other than the proposer) and
not by an external LLM self-report calibration paper; no such paper was found in this
search, a gap we note rather than paper over.

\subsection{Cost-aware agent systems}

Cost-aware serving reduces spend by routing or cascading across models of different
cost/quality at the level of a single query: an LLM cascade can achieve large cost
savings at matched or better accuracy than always using the strongest model
\citep{Chen2023}, and clustering queries to cheap models with escalation only for
low-confidence cases retains most of the strongest model's accuracy at a fraction of the
cost \citep{Moslem2026}. This hub's own cost-reduction work takes a different lever ---
trimming resent conversational context within a single fixed model per room, rather than
routing across models per query --- and has no escalation path: every seat in a room runs
the same model tier for the room's duration. Parallelized context compaction gives
fine-grained control over summary volume for long-running agent conversations
\citep{Cim2026}; the hub does not compact or summarize room history at all, since its
board exists precisely so long content lives outside chat rather than needing to be
summarized within it, an architectural choice we assert but do not measure against
compaction here. A related line maintains full dialogue history while generating
intent-aware summaries and compact tool logs to manage growing context in multi-turn
agents \citep{Su2026}; the hub's own incremental board-state synchronization solves a
narrower problem (delta delivery of board keys to a participant's cursor) than
intent-aware summarization of chat history.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology for agent benchmarks}

Strong LLMs used as judges reach over 80\% agreement with human preferences on
chat-assistant comparisons \citep{Zheng2023}; that figure is a \emph{preference}-agreement
ceiling, not a \emph{correctness} guarantee, and it is the reason this paper scores every
task by a hidden-fixture machine oracle rather than by an LLM judge. Two further findings
reinforce that choice: assigning asymmetric roles to a multi-agent panel of LLM judges
introduces a systematic downward bias that the panel's own consensus process does not
self-correct \citep{Song2026} --- a direct warning against ever using the hub's own
challenge/vote structure, which superficially resembles a panel of judges, as the scorer
of a benchmark task about the hub itself --- and rubric text can itself encode evaluative
signal, letting scores be predicted from the rubric independent of the candidate response
\citep{Bagaria2026}, so a rubric does not repair judge validity either. Synthetic
long-context benchmarks with simple needle-retrieval overstate real capability, and
richer needle configurations and multi-hop tasks are needed to resist this kind of gaming
\citep{Hsieh2024}; the analogous lesson for this paper's own task suite is that a fixture
answerable by one shallow pattern-match overstates what an oracle task measures, which is
part of why Section~\ref{sec:method} reports task admission as a rate over ten or more
seeds rather than a pass/fail on one.

\subsection{The narrow, honest contribution claim}

What we did not find in this search is a prior evaluation of a \emph{hub-enforced}
combination of blind-opening reveal, mandatory pre-quorum challenge, quote-verified agree
votes, a machine-parseable verification gate with a hub-assigned (not self-selected)
reviewer, and a supermajority electorate counted per connection, applied to LLM agent
coordination --- nor a benchmark of that combination against a budget-matched single
agent and an oracle-free multi-sample selection baseline on hidden-fixture,
machine-oracle tasks. That combination, and that comparison, is what this paper reports.
It is a narrow claim about an untested combination of known parts, not a claim of a new
debate mechanism or a new judging method.
