\subsection{Task suite and statistical unit}
\label{sec:results-suite}

The admitted primary suite (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}) is three tasks in two families, following the ``statistical unit is the family, not the instance'' rule fixed in \texttt{paper/amendments.md} (2026-09-18): \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \texttt{stamp-2} share one specification (the STAMP invented-language grammar) and one failure mechanism (a nested-scope closure read that contradicts the spec's single global-procedure-table rule), so they form the \emph{interpreter family}; \texttt{bench-printf-format} (exact reproduction of a host's shortest floating-point representation, single trap at \texttt{\%.17g} of \texttt{1e-07}) is its own family. Every task is a single-trap task: on each, every observed failure of arm A traces to one item or item pair, so a task's pass rate measures how often one slip happens, not a difficulty gradient (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}, \texttt{paper/amendments.md}). Consequently every significance test below is computed at the family level (pooling \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \texttt{stamp-2}), not per task; per-task counts are reported for transparency but are not treated as independent trials of ``task difficulty.''

Table~\ref{tab:family} (\texttt{paper/tables/results-family.tex}) is the primary RQ1 test: two-sided Fisher's exact test (never the two-proportion $z$-approximation; see below) on each family's pooled pass/fail counts, Holm-Bonferroni across the two families ($m=2$). On the printf family, arm A (14/40) outperforms arm C (6/40) numerically but \textbf{the difference is not significant}, Fisher $p=0.069$, Holm $p=0.069$.

\textbf{A methodological note the committed \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite/rq1-table.md} does not resolve on its own:} that file's per-task significance column follows \texttt{paper/protocol.md} \S5 literally (``two-proportion $z$-test, or Fisher's exact test when any cell count~$<5$''), and no cell of the printf $2\times2$ table is below 5, so it reports the $z$-test result for \texttt{bench-printf-format} A vs.\ C ($p=0.0389$, flagged significant). The $z$-test is a large-sample approximation; Fisher's exact test on the same $2\times2$ table gives $p=0.069$ (not significant), and this room's brief and \texttt{paper/amendments.md}'s arm-K precedent (``never the $z$-approximation switch'') both commit this paper to Fisher exact as the reported method. \textbf{We report the family-pooled Fisher result as the finding: printf shows no significant arm A vs.\ arm C difference.} The per-task $z$-test row is a distinct, weaker analysis (single task, not family-pooled, approximate test) that must not be read as the paper's printf conclusion; where both are shown, both are labelled by test name so a reader is not misled by which one used the word ``significant.''

\subsubsection*{Scoring \texorpdfstring{\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}}{the pre-registration} as written}

The pre-registration's Prediction~1 (``interpreter family: voting nearly closes the gap'') has three separate clauses, and they must each be scored, not summarized as a pass: (a) point forecasts of $\approx 0.99$ on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and $\approx 0.90$ on \texttt{stamp-2}; (b) K vs.\ C not significant on either task; (c) K vs.\ A significant on both tasks (``while arm K vs arm A is''). Clause (b) \textbf{holds} on both tasks. Clause (a) is \textbf{wrong in direction and magnitude} on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}: the forecast (0.99) was computed by resampling the 40 already-observed arm-A answers, whose pass rate (33/40 = 0.825) overstated the true single-agent rate; the real arm-K run reached 0.90 (36/40), and a fresh 400-attempt single-agent sample (\S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) passes at only 0.675. On \texttt{stamp-2} the point forecast (0.90) undershot: arm K reached 0.975 (39/40). Clause (c) \textbf{holds on \texttt{stamp-2} only} (Holm $p=0.009$) and \textbf{fails on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}} (Holm $p=1.000$, raw $p=0.518$): fresh single-agent attempts on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} passed often enough (0.675, \S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) that arm K's 0.90 is not statistically distinguishable from arm A's realized rate in this sample, even though it is numerically higher (36/40 vs.\ 33/40). Reporting only clauses (a)'s ``not significant'' half and ignoring the point forecast and the split clause-(c) result, as a summary might be tempted to, would overstate how cleanly the prediction held; we report all three clauses.

Prediction~2 (``printf: agreement-based selection does not help and may hurt'') \textbf{holds}: arm K passes 7/40 (0.175), at or below arm A's 14/40 (0.35) and close to arm C's 6/40 (0.15); the pre-run selector calibration (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``printf selector calibration'') predicted MBR-exec would select a passing candidate at 0.18--0.20 because the 40-candidate pool partitions into two signature-identical clusters that differ on only the known \texttt{1e-07} trap, and the correct cluster is the minority (14/40); the real run's 7/40 = 0.175 falls inside that predicted band. The oracle ceiling (any of the $k=7$ attempts passes; not an arm) is 37/40 = 0.925 (Table~\ref{tab:armk-explore}), so the gap between what selection achieves (0.175) and what is available to select (0.925) is the paper's clearest illustration that an agreement-rewarding selector actively hurts when the wrong answer is modal, rather than merely failing to help.

\subsection{Fresh attempt-level single-agent rates: how noisy $n=40$ was}
\label{sec:results-attempt-rates}

Because arm K spends $k$ fresh, non-communicating arm-A attempts per (task, seed), the arm-K grid incidentally produces far more single-agent attempts than the $n=40$ grid: 400 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 320 on \texttt{stamp-2}, 280 on \texttt{bench-printf-format}. Their pass rates (270/400 = 0.675, 261/320 = 0.816, 87/280 = 0.311) diverge from the earlier 40-run grid's rates (0.825, 0.700, 0.350) in both directions -- \texttt{stamp-interpreter} moved down 0.15, \texttt{stamp-2} moved up 0.116, printf moved down 0.039 -- which is the direct evidence that the original $n=40$ arm-A estimates, and hence the prereg's plug-in-resampling point forecasts derived from them (\S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}), were noisy in both directions, not merely optimistic. This is reported as a property of the sample size, not a claim that the process itself drifted (\S\ref{sec:results-window-control} addresses time-drift specifically for arm C).

\textbf{Cap-value control.} A candidate confound for the \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate drop is arm K's flat \$0.30 per-attempt budget cap, tighter than the grid's per-seed arm-A budget (paired to arm C's realized spend, mean \$0.058). \texttt{bench/results/rq1-drift-control} reruns arm A on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} at two fixed caps, 30 runs each, same afternoon: cap \$0.30 passed 23/30 (0.767); cap \$0.58 passed 18/30 (0.60). The tighter cap did not pass \emph{less} often -- if anything the reverse -- so a \$0.30 cap constraining an ordinarily 4--8-turn, 20--48-second attempt (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}) is not the cause of arm K's lower-than-forecast \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate; both cap values sit inside a single afternoon's rate variance.

\label{sec:results-window-control}

Arm C's 40/40 on both interpreter tasks (\S\ref{sec:results-per-task}) was measured in an earlier time window than arm K's confirmatory grid (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``Threat to validity this creates''). \texttt{bench/results/rq1-window-control} is designed to test this directly: 20 fresh seeds (201--220) of arm C and arm K interleaved \emph{per seed} on both interpreter tasks, so any time-dependent drift in the single-agent or chatroom rate affects both arms' measurement window equally. As stated above (auto-generated from the committed run directory at table-generation time), this control had not produced a complete 80-run set when this section was finalized, and per the room's instruction its partial results are not read as a result: the confound is reported as open, not resolved, and \textbf{arm C's 40/40 on the interpreter family should be read alongside this open time-window threat}, not as a settled ceiling.

\subsection{Cost per correct answer}
\label{sec:results-cost}

Cost per correct answer (Table~\ref{tab:per-task}, rightmost column; \texttt{usage.cost\_usd} summed over an arm's $n=40$ runs divided by its \texttt{task\_pass} count, \texttt{src/result.ts}'s \texttt{UsageRollup}) ranges from \$0.07--\$0.10 for arm A on the interpreter family to \$4.97 (arm C) and \$3.87 (arm K) on printf, where the denominator (6 and 7 correct answers respectively) is small. Arm C costs roughly 6--10$\times$ arm A per correct answer on the interpreter family despite a materially higher success rate (100\% vs.\ 70--82.5\%); arm K, budget-matched to arm C by construction (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}: realized per-seed spend never exceeds arm C's paired cost, verified in \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k/rq1-armk-table.md}'s cost table), costs comparably to arm C while reaching a lower success rate on two of three tasks. On printf, all three arms have a small, expensive numerator (14, 6 and 7 correct answers out of 40 attempts costing full-arm money each), which is why the cost-per-correct figures there are an order of magnitude higher than the interpreter family and should be read with that denominator in mind, not as a stable per-answer price. The cache-read accounting note in \S\ref{sec:figures-cost} (\texttt{paper/figures.md}: cache reads billed at roughly a tenth of fresh input) applies to every \texttt{cost\_usd} figure in this section, since Claude seats' \texttt{usage.cache\_read\_input\_tokens} dominates token volume on every arm-C and arm-K run (\texttt{docs/review-round-0918.md}: ``98 to 99\% of it cache reads'').

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

On the interpreter family, arm A's failures are near-identical: every \texttt{stamp-interpreter} arm-A failure in the confirmatory grid outputs 32 where 100 is expected (a nested-\texttt{DEF} lexical-closure misreading), and every \texttt{stamp-2} failure shares the analogous partial-closure misreading (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}, \texttt{paper/amendments.md}). Because the wrong answer is a single shared misreading rather than diverse noise, it is rarely the plurality among $k=8$--10 fresh attempts, so arm K's plurality vote recovers the correct answer in most groups; \texttt{paper/amendments.md} reports that all four of arm K's \texttt{stamp-interpreter} losses are groups where the shared wrong answer happened to be the plurality (4--5 of 10 attempts). Arm C's three-seat deliberation reaches the correct answer on every group (80/80 pooled) via the same underlying mechanism from the debate side: a seat that reasons through the scope rule independently, or is corrected by a challenge, breaks the shared misreading before a vote is needed. On printf the situation inverts: the wrong \texttt{\%.17g} formatting is the \emph{modal} behaviour among independent attempts (26/40 arm-A failures, all the same case), so both an aggregation method that rewards agreement (arm K's MBR-exec, \S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}) and a three-seat deliberation that must also reach agreement to conclude (arm C) do worse than one unaggregated attempt (arm A 14/40 vs.\ arm K 7/40 vs.\ arm C 6/40). The common thread is not ``more agents help'' or ``voting helps''; it is whether the shared failure mode is a minority or a majority position among independent attempts, which the paper cannot determine without running the single-agent distribution first (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).
