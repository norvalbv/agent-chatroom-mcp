\subsection{Study 1: conversation and independent selection}
\label{sec:results-study1}

Table~\ref{tab:study1} gives the A--C family comparison; Figures~\ref{fig:pass-rate} to~\ref{fig:vote-mechanism} show the same Study 1 results as charts. Across the two interpreter programs, A passed 61/80 and C passed 80/80. The family-pooled Fisher test, adopted after the runs, gives $p=0.000001$ before Holm adjustment. The original pre-registered per-task tests also rejected A--C on each interpreter program; the pooled result is a revised analysis, not the pre-registered test. K passed 36/40 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and 39/40 on \texttt{stamp-2}, or 75/80 pooled. Its pooled comparison with A ($p=0.0033$) is exploratory. The registered K tests show a narrower result: K beat A on \texttt{stamp-2} (Holm $p=0.009$), but was not distinguished from A on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} (Holm $p=1.000$). The pre-registered prediction said K would beat A on both. That clause failed.

Direct K--C tests did not distinguish the arms on any Study 1 task: every Holm-adjusted $p$ was at least $0.578$. Pooling the interpreter tasks gives 75/80 for K and 80/80 for C, with an exploratory Fisher $p=0.059$. Neither result establishes equality. In particular, a significant C--A result and a nonsignificant K--A result on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} are not themselves a test of C against K. The direct comparison is the relevant test, and the available sample leaves a small difference unresolved.

On \texttt{bench-printf-format}, A passed 14/40, C 6/40, and K 7/40. The pre-registered A--C z-test gave $p=0.039$; the later family-based Fisher test gave $p=0.069$, so the significance decision depends on the stated test. K was not distinguished from A (Holm $p=0.578$) or C. The observed rates on this task are lower for both aggregation arms; they do not support a general assertion that either method lowers success on other tasks.

The independent-attempt evidence explains K's printf outcome more specifically. In a fixed pool of 40 A attempts, all 14 passing implementations agreed on every one of 9,650 public-specification probes, and all 26 failing implementations also agreed with one another. Thus execution agreement identified the larger wrong cluster. In the real K groups, at least one correct attempt was available in 37/40, but the oracle-free selector submitted a correct one in only 7/40. The availability count is an oracle ceiling, not a deployable arm. The C artifacts retain pass or fail, but not the failing implementations; the same modal-error explanation for C is an association, not an observed C selection mechanism.

The interpreter answers show the opposite population pattern in Study 1. For \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, the seven A failures among 40 were identical; for \texttt{stamp-2}, 11 of the 12 failures were identical and one differed. Here the correct answer was the majority of A answers in the observed pool. The room summaries add diagnostic context: at least 26 of 40 \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rooms and 14 of 40 \texttt{stamp-2} rooms reported an erroneous opening followed by another seat's correction. These are manual lower bounds from final seat summaries, without retained raw room events. They do not identify a causal chatroom mechanic.



The extra computation had a substantial price in these runs. C's cost per correct answer relative to A was 8.56 times on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 6.16 times on \texttt{stamp-2}, and 18.17 times on printf. K and C cost respectively \$0.637 and \$0.602 per correct answer on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, \$0.545 and \$0.595 on \texttt{stamp-2}, and \$3.87 and \$4.97 on printf. These are list-price calculations under the recorded accounts and include all K attempts, including those not selected.

\subsection{Account-associated thinking}
\label{sec:results-account}

Table~\ref{tab:account} joins single-seat run times to the sanitized switch log. Among runs with thinking telemetry, account 4 had 384/385 at or above 4,000 thinking tokens. Accounts 1 and 3 had none among 51 combined runs. Median thinking was 7,709 tokens on account 4, against 968 and 716 on accounts 1 and 3. Within each of the two task families shown, account 4's output-token range lay entirely above those of the other measured accounts. These are strong associations under the same alias and settings, not evidence that the client can identify what the provider changed.

The association matters for interpreting accuracy. Short-thinking single-agent measurements on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} came from several cohorts: the admission pilot, the Study 1 A grid, constituent K attempts, a cap control, and the window control. Their estimates ranged from $0.475$ to $0.825$. The Study 1 grid's 33/40 was therefore one estimate within an observed span, not a stable model parameter. The separate long-thinking window-control cohort had 78/80 successful constituent single attempts. Study 2's long-thinking A arm passed 18/19 on that task and cost \$0.136 per correct answer. These are different cohorts, not a randomized account comparison; their gap does not show that either account or effort caused an accuracy change. The source of the variation among short-thinking estimates is also unidentified. \texttt{stamp-2} was not measured under long thinking, so the long-thinking interpreter result is confined to \texttt{stamp-interpreter}.

Within the exploratory long-thinking stratum, the interpreter comparisons use 19 seeds per arm. A passed 18/19; AH, B, K and C each passed 19/19. \subsection{How seats check each other}
\label{sec:results-review}

This subsection is an exploratory audit added after both studies; it was not pre-registered. It asks what one seat's check of another seat's work consisted of. It covers every verification write whose first line is a JSON object in the hub's logs of rooms and sub-rooms created before 10:00 UTC on 23 September 2026: 260 writes from 36 rooms created between 17 and 23 September, of which 24 worked on this system's code, research or documentation, 10 on browser games and 2 on another codebase. These rooms used a mix of Claude, Codex and DeepSeek models; they are not the benchmark runs of Studies 1 and 2.

\paragraph{What the gate asks for.} In a room that requires verification, a proposal can pass only once a qualifying verification entry exists. The entry must use a verification key not marked partial and begin with a parseable head: string fields for the proposal, the command, its working directory and the tail of its output, a numeric exit code of 0, and a commit if one is given. It must come from a seat other than the proposer, on a different connection, and be no older than the proposal's current text; while a reviewer assigned by the hub remains in the room, only that reviewer's entry counts. The gate does not ask what the command exercised or whether it differs from the author's own check, and it cannot establish that the command ran: the head is a structured report. An entry reporting a failure cannot satisfy the gate; only a separate path for executable challenges lets a verifier rule a challenger's command invalid at a nonzero exit code, with a stated reason. When verification is required and the board holds no verification entry at all, the hub refuses a proposal and asks the author to post the command they ran, adding that ``someone else must then run it''. When an open proposal still needs a blocking challenge and a seat has not yet voted, that seat may receive a hint calling the proposal ``open and untested'' and asking for its weakest claim in one sentence. A passing report of the author's own command therefore satisfies the gate. The gate supplies nothing like the held-out tests applied during SWE-bench evaluation~\cite{Jimenez2023}.

\paragraph{What verification entries report.} Table~\ref{tab:verify-practice} and Figure~\ref{fig:review-practice}a classify each entry by the strongest check its text reports. Two same-model AI coders labelled all 260 entries independently from their full text and agreed on 256 (Cohen's $\kappa=0.98$); an adjudicator resolved the other four. Agreement does not establish correctness: an independent review of 48 entries found three labels that both coders had got wrong, and they are corrected here. Of the 260 entries, 241 parse under the hub's own head parser, 238 report exit code 0 and 128 name the commit they checked; 110 were written by a seat other than the proposal's known author, 11 by the author, and for 139 the author could not be resolved. Most entries report re-running existing checks: 126 only builds, type checks or existing tests, and 43 the scripted smoke client, which drives a local hub with model spawning disabled; 10 report only reading code or history. A substantial minority report more. Fifty-five report an added check, either a probe the verifier wrote or a before/after run such as a regression test run against the unfixed code, which may reuse existing tests; 26, all in game rooms, report driving the built application in a real browser. No entry reports its writer running model-driven agents on a changed build, though that does not establish that none ran. Among the 110 entries by a seat other than the known proposal author, 34/110 report an added check and 11/110 a browser run.

\paragraph{What review acts found.} In four of these rooms, two on this system and two game rooms, one AI auditor catalogued review acts from the room log and each seat's own transcript and classified each by method. A second auditor re-counted each room: it found acts the first had missed, disputed some classifications, and flagged repeated reports and self-checks (Table~\ref{tab:review-audit}, Figure~\ref{fig:review-practice}b). In the first auditor's coding, re-running the author's test, suite or build was the largest category, 154 of 331 acts, and had the lowest defect-flagged share among categories with a stated method: 39/154, against 42/73 for reading the diff and 25/38 for a check the author had not supplied. These are associations. They do not show why a reviewer chose a method or which method produced a finding, and a reviewer may write its own check because it already suspects a problem.

In the first room on this system, the author of a benchmark pilot ran model agents on an integration build containing other seats' changes; the auditors concluded that the pilot did not meaningfully test draft sealing and found no reviewer inspecting its logs. In the second, neither auditor found a reviewer running model agents on a changed build. Across both, the acts the first auditor counted as using the system ``as its users would'' were scripted smoke checks, observations of the unchanged live hub, and one changed-build launcher check that stopped at a refusal. The second auditor estimated about 35 requests about test evidence in the first room, mostly for re-runnable commands or scope caveats; requests led to the pilot on the integration build, and a later discussion ended in a decision not to re-run it on the final head. In the game rooms, non-author seats did open the game, at least 8 and 15 times by the auditors' counts. The first auditor listed 12 and 10 items that lacked relevant non-author exercise in the game, some grouping several changes; the re-counts corrected several items, including a pre-merge browser check of one game's residents change. That check covered night scenes only, and the owner reported the change failing in daylight about six minutes after its merge was announced.
