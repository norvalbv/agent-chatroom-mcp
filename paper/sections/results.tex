\subsection{Task suite and statistical unit}
\label{sec:results-suite}

The admitted primary suite (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}) is three tasks in two families, following the ``statistical unit is the family, not the instance'' rule fixed in \texttt{paper/amendments.md} (2026-09-18): \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \texttt{stamp-2} share one specification (the STAMP invented-language grammar) and one failure mechanism (a nested-scope closure read that contradicts the spec's single global-procedure-table rule), so they form the \emph{interpreter family}; \texttt{bench-printf-format} (exact reproduction of a host's shortest floating-point representation, single trap at \texttt{\%.17g} of \texttt{1e-07}) is its own family. Every task is a single-trap task: on each, nearly every observed failure of arm A traces to one item or item pair, so a task's pass rate measures how often one slip happens, not a difficulty gradient (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}, \texttt{paper/amendments.md}). The primary arm A vs.\ arm C test (\S\ref{sec:results-a-vs-c}) is computed at this family level. Arm K's confirmatory test (\S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}) keeps its own pre-registered family instead: \texttt{paper/amendments.md} fixed the Holm-Bonferroni family for arm K at the \emph{per-task} level ($m=6$: 3 tasks $\times$ \{K vs.\ C, K vs.\ A\}) before any arm-K run existed, and this paper honors that pre-registration rather than re-pooling it; \S\ref{sec:results-arm-k-family} reports the family-pooled arm-K numbers separately, labelled exploratory.

\label{sec:results-a-vs-c}

\label{tab:family}

Table~\ref{tab:family} (\texttt{paper/tables/results-family.tex}) is the primary RQ1 test: two-sided Fisher's exact test (never the two-proportion $z$-approximation; see below) on each family's pooled pass/fail counts, Holm-Bonferroni across the two families ($m=2$). On the interpreter family, arm C (80/80) is significantly better than arm A (61/80 pooled across \texttt{stamp-interpreter} 33/40 and \texttt{stamp-2} 28/40), Holm $p<0.001$. On the printf family, arm A (14/40) outperforms arm C (6/40) numerically but \textbf{the difference is not significant}, Fisher $p=0.069$, Holm $p=0.069$.

\textbf{Disclosed deviation from the written pre-registration.} \texttt{paper/protocol.md} \S5, as pre-registered, selects the test per task: ``two-proportion $z$-test, or Fisher's exact test when any cell count~$<5$.'' No cell of the printf $2\times2$ table is below 5, so the pre-registered method is the $z$-test, and the committed \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite/rq1-table.md} reports exactly that: $p=0.0389$, flagged significant. This paper instead reports Fisher's exact test on the same $2\times2$ table ($p=0.069$, not significant) and pools \texttt{stamp-interpreter}+\texttt{stamp-2} into one family before testing, neither of which is what \S5 as written specifies for this comparison (\S5 does not mention family pooling for arm A vs.\ arm C at all; the family-pooling rule was added later, in the \texttt{paper/amendments.md} suite-admission entry, for task selection, and this paper extends it to the significance test by analogy, after the data existed). \textbf{This is a forking path, disclosed here rather than silently substituted}: both results are reported side by side, by test name, and neither is called ``the finding'' at the expense of hiding the other. The pre-registered per-task $z$-test says printf A vs.\ C is significant at $p=0.039$; the family-pooled Fisher test this paper adopted post hoc says it is not, $p=0.069$. A reader who holds this paper to its written pre-registration should treat the $z$-test result as the pre-registered one and the Fisher/family result as an exploratory revision, not the reverse.

\subsubsection*{Scoring \texorpdfstring{\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}}{the pre-registration} as written}

The pre-registration's Prediction~1 (``interpreter family: voting nearly closes the gap'') has three separate clauses, and they must each be scored, not summarized as a pass: (a) point forecasts of $\approx 0.99$ on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and $\approx 0.90$ on \texttt{stamp-2}; (b) K vs.\ C not significant on either task; (c) K vs.\ A significant on both tasks (``while arm K vs arm A is''). Clause (b) \textbf{holds} on both tasks. Clause (a) is \textbf{wrong in magnitude on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}} (both the forecast and the outcome are on the same side of the gap between arm A and arm C, so this is not a direction error): the forecast (0.99) was computed by resampling the 40 already-observed arm-A answers, whose pass rate (33/40 = 0.825) overstated the true single-agent rate; the real arm-K run reached 0.90 (36/40), and a fresh 400-attempt single-agent sample (\S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) passes at only 0.675. On \texttt{stamp-2} the point forecast (0.90) undershot: arm K reached 0.975 (39/40). Clause (c) \textbf{holds on \texttt{stamp-2} only} (Holm $p=0.009$) and \textbf{fails on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}} (Holm $p=1.000$, raw $p=0.518$): fresh single-agent attempts on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} passed often enough (0.675, \S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) that arm K's 0.90 is not statistically distinguishable from arm A's realized rate in this sample, even though it is numerically higher (36/40 vs.\ 33/40). Reporting only clause (b)'s ``not significant'' result and ignoring the point forecast and the split clause-(c) result, as a summary might be tempted to, would overstate how cleanly the prediction held; we report all three clauses.

Prediction~2 (``printf: agreement-based selection does not help and may hurt'') \textbf{holds}: arm K passes 7/40 (0.175), at or below arm A's 14/40 (0.35) and close to arm C's 6/40 (0.15); the pre-run selector calibration (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``printf selector calibration'') estimated MBR-exec would select a passing candidate at about 0.18--0.20 (two closed forms, hypergeometric 0.1789 and binomial 0.1998) by resampling the 40 already-finished arm-A candidates, because that pool partitions into two signature-identical clusters that differ on only the known \texttt{1e-07} trap and the correct cluster is the minority (14/40). \texttt{paper/amendments.md} is explicit that this calibration is \emph{not} a prediction of the real arm-K run, whose $k=7$ attempts are fresh draws from the underlying process, not resamples of these 40; the real run's 7/40 = 0.175 is close to, but strictly below, the calibration's lower closed form (0.1789). The oracle ceiling (any of the $k=7$ attempts passes; not an arm) is 37/40 = 0.925 (Table~\ref{tab:armk-explore}), so the gap between what selection achieves (0.175) and what is available to select (0.925) is the paper's clearest illustration that an agreement-rewarding selector actively hurts when the wrong answer is modal, rather than merely failing to help.

\subsection{Fresh attempt-level single-agent rates: how noisy $n=40$ was}
\label{sec:results-attempt-rates}

Because arm K spends $k$ fresh, non-communicating arm-A attempts per (task, seed), the arm-K grid incidentally produces far more single-agent attempts than the $n=40$ grid: 400 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 320 on \texttt{stamp-2}, 280 on \texttt{bench-printf-format}. Their pass rates (270/400 = 0.675, 261/320 = 0.816, 87/280 = 0.311) diverge from the earlier 40-run grid's rates (0.825, 0.700, 0.350) in both directions -- \texttt{stamp-interpreter} moved down 0.15, \texttt{stamp-2} moved up 0.116, printf moved down 0.039 -- which is the direct evidence that the original $n=40$ arm-A estimates, and hence the prereg's plug-in-resampling point forecasts derived from them (\S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}), were noisy in both directions, not merely optimistic. This is reported as a property of the sample size, not a claim that the process itself drifted (\S\ref{sec:results-window-control} addresses time-drift specifically for arm C).

\textbf{Cap-value control.} A candidate confound for the \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate drop is arm K's flat \$0.30 per-attempt budget cap: each arm-K attempt runs under a fixed \$0.30 ceiling (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``Arm K, settled definition''), tighter than the grid's arm-A budget on this task, which is matched \emph{per seed} to arm C's realized spend and averages \$0.602 (Table~\ref{tab:per-task}) -- i.e.\ roughly double the flat cap, not below it. \texttt{bench/results/rq1-drift-control} reruns arm A on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} at two fixed caps, 30 runs each, same afternoon: cap \$0.30 passed 23/30 (0.767); cap \$0.58 passed 18/30 (0.60), a cap value close to the grid's own mean arm-C-matched budget. The tighter cap did not pass \emph{less} often -- if anything the reverse -- so the \$0.30 flat cap constraining an ordinarily 4--8-turn, 20--48-second attempt (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}) is not the cause of arm K's lower-than-forecast \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate; both cap values sit inside a single afternoon's rate variance.

\subsection{Cost per correct answer}
\label{sec:results-cost}

Cost per correct answer (Table~\ref{tab:per-task}, rightmost column; \texttt{usage.cost\_usd} summed over an arm's $n=40$ runs divided by its \texttt{task\_pass} count, \texttt{src/result.ts}'s \texttt{UsageRollup}) ranges from \$0.07--\$0.10 for arm A on the interpreter family to \$4.97 (arm C) and \$3.87 (arm K) on printf, where the denominator (6 and 7 correct answers respectively) is small. Arm C costs roughly 6.2--8.6$\times$ arm A per correct answer on the interpreter family (\$0.6018/\$0.0703 = 8.56$\times$ on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}; \$0.5946/\$0.0965 = 6.16$\times$ on \texttt{stamp-2}) despite a materially higher success rate (100\% vs.\ 70--82.5\%). Arm K is budget-matched to arm C \emph{on average}, not on every seed: \texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}'s matching rule is judged on realized \emph{mean} per-seed spend, and \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k/rq1-armk-table.md}'s cost table confirms the mean is at or below arm C's paired mean on all three tasks -- but per seed, arm K's cost exceeds its paired arm-C cost on 19/40 \texttt{stamp-interpreter} seeds, 10/40 \texttt{stamp-2} seeds and 9/40 \texttt{bench-printf-format} seeds (counted directly from \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k} and \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite} result files); ``budget-matched'' in this paper means the mean, never a per-seed guarantee. On printf, all three arms have a small, expensive numerator (14, 6 and 7 correct answers out of 40 attempts costing full-arm money each), which is why the cost-per-correct figures there are an order of magnitude higher than the interpreter family and should be read with that denominator in mind, not as a stable per-answer price. Cache reads dominate token volume on every arm-C run: computed directly from Table~\ref{tab:per-task}'s own mean-token columns (cache-read tokens divided by input$+$cache-read$+$cache-creation tokens, the formula \texttt{paper/figures.md} and \texttt{scripts/claude-room-usage.py} use), arm C's cache-read share is about 94.6\% on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 94.2\% on \texttt{stamp-2} and 93.6\% on \texttt{bench-printf-format} -- close to, but not identical to, the 98--99\% figure \texttt{docs/review-round-0918.md} reports for build-room seats, which measured a different (non-benchmark) workload and should not be quoted as this paper's own number. Cache reads are billed at roughly a tenth of fresh input price (\texttt{paper/figures.md}), so this high share is why arm C and arm K's dollar cost grows much more slowly than their raw token counts would suggest.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

On the interpreter family, arm A's failures are highly concentrated but not uniform. Every one of the 7 \texttt{stamp-interpreter} arm-A failures in the confirmatory grid (seeds 101--140) answers 101 where 100 is expected, in the same trailing position of the output (a nested-\texttt{DEF} lexical-closure misreading; verified directly against each failing seed's \texttt{workspace/answer.txt}, not taken from the task-admission pilot note in \texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}, which describes a different, smaller pilot program and reports ``32'' for that program, not this one). \texttt{stamp-2}'s 12 arm-A failures are not one shared answer: 11 give ``1027 1008'' for the expected ``3007 3005'' (the same partial-closure misreading at one output position) and 1 gives only ``1008'' wrong, matching the other 11 on the first of the two positions but not the second (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}: ``11 one identical wrong answer, 1 another''; confirmed directly against \texttt{workspace/answer.txt}). Because the dominant wrong answer, on both tasks, is a single shared misreading rather than diverse noise, it is rarely the plurality among $k=8$--10 fresh attempts, so arm K's plurality vote recovers the correct answer in most groups; \texttt{paper/amendments.md} reports that all four of arm K's \texttt{stamp-interpreter} losses are groups where the shared wrong answer happened to be the plurality (4--5 of 10 attempts). Arm C's three-seat deliberation reaches the correct answer on every group (80/80 pooled). \textbf{Why is untested}: a plausible account is that a seat reasoning through the scope rule independently, or a challenge that surfaces the disagreement before a vote, breaks the shared misreading -- but no transcript analysis in this paper's evidence base checks this, so it is stated here as a hypothesis, not a measured mechanism. On printf, arm A's wrong \texttt{\%.17g} formatting is the \emph{modal} behaviour among independent attempts (the complement of its 14/40 pass rate, Table~\ref{tab:per-task}; every one of those failures is the same case per \texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}'s single-trap record), so both an aggregation method that rewards agreement (arm K's MBR-exec, \S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}) and a three-seat deliberation that must also reach agreement to conclude (arm C) do worse than one unaggregated attempt (arm A 14/40 vs.\ arm K 7/40 vs.\ arm C 6/40). Whether arm C's 34 printf failures share the same \texttt{1e-07} case as arm A's is \textbf{not recorded}: \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite}'s arm-C results carry only an oracle pass/fail exit code, not which hidden case failed, so this paper does not claim they are the same failure, only that both arms fail at a similar rate on the same known single-trap task. The common thread this section can support is that whether the shared failure mode is a minority or a majority position among independent attempts predicts whether aggregation (voting or deliberation) helps or hurts; \emph{why} deliberation specifically tracks that same pattern, rather than only sampling-based voting, is discussed as an open question in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
