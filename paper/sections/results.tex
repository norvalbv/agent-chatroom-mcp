\subsection{Task suite and statistical unit}
\label{sec:results-suite}

The admitted primary suite (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}) is three tasks in two families, following the ``statistical unit is the family, not the instance'' rule fixed in \texttt{paper/amendments.md} (2026-09-18): \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \texttt{stamp-2} share one specification (the STAMP invented-language grammar) and one failure mechanism (a nested-scope closure read that contradicts the spec's single global-procedure-table rule), so they form the \emph{interpreter family}; \texttt{bench-printf-format} (exact reproduction of a host's shortest floating-point representation, single trap at \texttt{\%.17g} of \texttt{1e-07}) is its own family. Every task is a single-trap task: on each, nearly every observed failure of arm A traces to one item or item pair, so a task's pass rate measures how often one slip happens, not a difficulty gradient (\texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}, \texttt{paper/amendments.md}). The primary arm A vs.\ arm C test (\S\ref{sec:results-a-vs-c}) is computed at this family level. Arm K's confirmatory test (\S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}) keeps its own pre-registered family instead: \texttt{paper/amendments.md} fixed the Holm-Bonferroni family for arm K at the \emph{per-task} level ($m=6$: 3 tasks $\times$ \{K vs.\ C, K vs.\ A\}) before any arm-K run existed, and this paper honors that pre-registration rather than re-pooling it; \S\ref{sec:results-arm-k-family} reports the family-pooled arm-K numbers separately, labelled exploratory.

Table~\ref{tab:family} (\texttt{paper/tables/results-family.tex}) is the primary RQ1 test: two-sided Fisher's exact test (never the two-proportion $z$-approximation; see below) on each family's pooled pass/fail counts, Holm-Bonferroni across the two families ($m=2$). On the interpreter family, arm C (80/80) is significantly better than arm A (61/80 pooled across \texttt{stamp-interpreter} 33/40 and \texttt{stamp-2} 28/40), Holm $p<0.001$. On the printf family, arm A (14/40) outperforms arm C (6/40) numerically but \textbf{the difference is not significant}, Fisher $p=0.069$, Holm $p=0.069$.

\textbf{Disclosed deviation from the written pre-registration.} \texttt{paper/protocol.md} \S5, as pre-registered, selects the test per task: ``two-proportion $z$-test, or Fisher's exact test when any cell count~$<5$.'' No cell of the printf $2\times2$ table is below 5, so the pre-registered method is the $z$-test, and the committed \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite/rq1-table.md} reports exactly that: $p=0.0389$, flagged significant. This paper instead reports Fisher's exact test on the same $2\times2$ table ($p=0.069$, not significant) and pools \texttt{stamp-interpreter}+\texttt{stamp-2} into one family before testing, neither of which is what \S5 as written specifies for this comparison (\S5 does not mention family pooling for arm A vs.\ arm C at all; the family-pooling rule was added later, in the \texttt{paper/amendments.md} suite-admission entry, for task selection, and this paper extends it to the significance test by analogy, after the data existed). \textbf{This is a forking path, disclosed here rather than silently substituted}: both results are reported side by side, by test name, and neither is called ``the finding'' at the expense of hiding the other. The pre-registered per-task $z$-test says printf A vs.\ C is significant at $p=0.039$; the family-pooled Fisher test this paper adopted post hoc says it is not, $p=0.069$. A reader who holds this paper to its written pre-registration should treat the $z$-test result as the pre-registered one and the Fisher/family result as an exploratory revision, not the reverse.

\subsubsection*{Scoring \texorpdfstring{\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}}{the pre-registration} as written}

The pre-registration's Prediction~1 (``interpreter family: voting nearly closes the gap'') has three separate clauses, and they must each be scored, not summarized as a pass: (a) point forecasts of $\approx 0.99$ on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and $\approx 0.90$ on \texttt{stamp-2}; (b) K vs.\ C not significant on either task; (c) K vs.\ A significant on both tasks (``while arm K vs arm A is''). Clause (b) \textbf{holds} on both tasks. Clause (a) is \textbf{wrong in magnitude on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}} (both the forecast and the outcome are on the same side of the gap between arm A and arm C, so this is not a direction error): the forecast (0.99) was computed by resampling the 40 already-observed arm-A answers, whose pass rate (33/40 = 0.825) overstated the true single-agent rate; the real arm-K run reached 0.90 (36/40), and a fresh 400-attempt single-agent sample (\S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) passes at only 0.675. On \texttt{stamp-2} the point forecast (0.90) undershot: arm K reached 0.975 (39/40). Clause (c) \textbf{holds on \texttt{stamp-2} only} (Holm $p=0.009$) and \textbf{fails on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}} (Holm $p=1.000$, raw $p=0.518$): fresh single-agent attempts on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} passed often enough (0.675, \S\ref{sec:results-attempt-rates}) that arm K's 0.90 is not statistically distinguishable from arm A's realized rate in this sample, even though it is numerically higher (36/40 vs.\ 33/40). Reporting only clause (b)'s ``not significant'' result and ignoring the point forecast and the split clause-(c) result, as a summary might be tempted to, would overstate how cleanly the prediction held; we report all three clauses.

Prediction~2 (``printf: agreement-based selection does not help and may hurt'') \textbf{holds}: arm K passes 7/40 (0.175), at or below arm A's 14/40 (0.35) and close to arm C's 6/40 (0.15); the pre-run selector calibration (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``printf selector calibration'') estimated MBR-exec would select a passing candidate at about 0.18--0.20 (two closed forms, hypergeometric 0.1789 and binomial 0.1998) by resampling the 40 already-finished arm-A candidates, because that pool partitions into two signature-identical clusters that differ on only the known \texttt{1e-07} trap and the correct cluster is the minority (14/40). \texttt{paper/amendments.md} is explicit that this calibration is \emph{not} a prediction of the real arm-K run, whose $k=7$ attempts are fresh draws from the underlying process, not resamples of these 40; the real run's 7/40 = 0.175 is close to, but strictly below, the calibration's lower closed form (0.1789). The oracle ceiling (any of the $k=7$ attempts passes; not an arm) is 37/40 = 0.925 (Table~\ref{tab:armk-explore}), so the gap between what selection achieves (0.175) and what is available to select (0.925) is the paper's clearest illustration that an agreement-rewarding selector actively hurts when the wrong answer is modal, rather than merely failing to help.

\subsection{Fresh attempt-level single-agent rates: how noisy $n=40$ was}
\label{sec:results-attempt-rates}

Because arm K spends $k$ fresh, non-communicating arm-A attempts per (task, seed), the arm-K grid incidentally produces far more single-agent attempts than the $n=40$ grid: 400 on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 320 on \texttt{stamp-2}, 280 on \texttt{bench-printf-format}. Their pass rates (270/400 = 0.675, 261/320 = 0.816, 87/280 = 0.311) diverge from the earlier 40-run grid's rates (0.825, 0.700, 0.350) in both directions -- \texttt{stamp-interpreter} moved down 0.15, \texttt{stamp-2} moved up 0.116, printf moved down 0.039 -- which is the direct evidence that the original $n=40$ arm-A estimates, and hence the prereg's plug-in-resampling point forecasts derived from them (\S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}), were noisy in both directions, not merely optimistic. This is reported as a property of the sample size, not a claim that the process itself drifted (\S\ref{sec:results-window-control} addresses time-drift specifically for arm C).

\textbf{Cap-value control.} A candidate confound for the \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate drop is arm K's flat \$0.30 per-attempt budget cap: each arm-K attempt runs under a fixed \$0.30 ceiling (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``Arm K, settled definition''), tighter than the grid's arm-A budget on this task, which is matched \emph{per seed} to arm C's realized spend and averages \$0.6018, the paired mean arm-C cost \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k/rq1-armk-table.md} reports for \texttt{stamp-interpreter} -- i.e.\ roughly double the flat cap, not below it. \texttt{bench/results/rq1-drift-control} reruns arm A on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} at two fixed caps, 30 runs each, same afternoon: cap \$0.30 passed 23/30 (0.767); cap \$0.58 passed 18/30 (0.60), a cap value close to the grid's own mean arm-C-matched budget. The tighter cap did not pass \emph{less} often -- if anything the reverse -- so the \$0.30 flat cap constraining an ordinarily 4--8-turn, 20--48-second attempt (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}) is not the cause of arm K's lower-than-forecast \texttt{stamp-interpreter} rate; both cap values sit inside a single afternoon's rate variance.

\subsection{Window control}
\label{sec:results-window-control}

\input{tables/window-control-status}

Arm C's 40/40 on both interpreter tasks (\S\ref{sec:results-per-task}) was measured in an earlier time window than arm K's confirmatory grid (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``Threat to validity this creates''). This threat sharpened while this paper was being written: a separate 2026-09-19 amendment (\texttt{paper/amendments.md}, ``The model behind the \texttt{sonnet} alias changed its thinking budget mid-study'') reports that, with no harness, flag, project-setting or Claude Code version change, a single-agent \texttt{stamp-interpreter} attempt's output-token count rose about sixfold and its cost about three to four and a half times between about 15:30 and 16:45 local time on 2026-09-19 (about 1.6K output tokens / \$0.058, stable before that window across the arm-A/C grid, the arm-K attempts and the cap control alike, vs.\ about 10K output tokens / \$0.18--\$0.26 afterward; arm C seats show the same shift, 0.60 USD before vs.\ 1.50--2.21 USD after). This is a pre/post-shift distinction by output-token count, not a ``morning vs.\ afternoon'' one: \S\ref{sec:results-cost} gives the exact run windows, since the arm-K grid itself ran in the afternoon and partly overlaps the shift window on the clock without showing the shift in its own output tokens. \textbf{Every comparison in this paper is therefore valid only within its own measurement window}, and the original window-control design (20 fresh seeds 201--220, arm C and arm K interleaved per seed on both interpreter tasks, \texttt{bench/results/rq1-window-control}) was stopped after 3 runs (\$5.76) when the new per-run cost made the planned 80 runs exceed the control's own cost cap -- itself now evidence for the regime shift, not a result on the original question. The redesigned control completed at reduced scope: \texttt{stamp-interpreter} only, seeds 201--212, in the same directory (log \texttt{logs/rq1-window-control-2.log}; committed in \texttt{c82124d}). It did not remain within one regime: the output-token scale changed back during the control. The generated table above separates the long-output cohort, the crossover seed, and the short-output cohort. In the crossover, arm~C completed before the boundary while the corresponding arm~K attempts completed after it; assigning both arms to the latter regime would hide this mismatch. The single-attempt column counts constituent arm~K attempts and is not an independently randomized arm-A condition. Long-output attempts were near ceiling on this task, while the short-output cohort remained fallible; these are descriptive observations across time, not a randomized estimate of the effect of thinking budget. The small within-cohort samples do not resolve the original arm-C-vs-arm-K time-window comparison or establish equivalence. The earlier perfect arm-C record should be read alongside this completed control's observed failure, and task admission must be checked for the model and observed regime in which a comparison is run.

During the confirmatory run the active account reached its five-hour usage limit, the command-line client

(Table in \S\ref{sec:results-window-control}) are the two sides of one switch, and its crossover seed is
the seed whose runs straddle it.

Three consequences follow. First, the largest accuracy difference measured in this project, the
single-agent gap between the short and long cohorts of the window control, is a difference between two
accounts' serving conditions, not between two dates or two model versions. Second, the original RQ1 grid
and arm~K ran entirely under one short-thinking account, so their comparisons are internally consistent.
Third, what differs between the accounts is not observable from the client, so this paper reports the
effect and not its cause. The quota incident itself contaminated nine cells, which were excluded by a
rule that does not look at outcomes (any seat answered by the client's synthetic quota message) and
re-run; the rule, the excluded cells and the harness fix are in \texttt{paper/amendments.md}
(2026-09-20 08:00 UTC entry).

\subsection{Cost per correct answer}
\label{sec:results-cost}

Cost per correct answer, mean output tokens per run and the collection date range for every (task, arm) cell are in Table~\ref{tab:cost-per-correct}, not repeated here; that table's own caption carries the regime disclosure (output tokens, not clock time, is the pre/post-shift indicator, and arm K's clock-time overlap with the shift window does not put it in the post-shift regime by that indicator). This subsection reports only the derived comparisons Table~\ref{tab:cost-per-correct} does not already state.

Arm C costs \$0.6018 per correct answer on \texttt{stamp-interpreter} and \$0.5946 on \texttt{stamp-2} against arm A's \$0.0703 and \$0.0965 (Table~\ref{tab:cost-per-correct}) -- 8.56$\times$ and 6.16$\times$ respectively (\texttt{paper/generated/rq1-family-table.json}'s \texttt{costRatios}, computed by \texttt{scripts/paper-rq1-family.ts} from the same figures) -- despite a materially higher success rate (100\% vs.\ 70--82.5\%). Arm K is budget-matched to arm C \emph{on average}, not on every seed: \texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}'s matching rule is judged on realized \emph{mean} per-seed spend, and \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k/rq1-armk-table.md}'s cost table confirms the mean is at or below arm C's paired mean on all three tasks -- but per seed, arm K's cost exceeds its paired arm-C cost on 19 of 40 \texttt{stamp-interpreter} seeds, 10 of 40 \texttt{stamp-2} seeds and 9 of 40 \texttt{bench-printf-format} seeds (\texttt{paper/generated/rq1-family-table.json}'s \texttt{kExceedsC}, computed by the same script from \texttt{bench/results/rq1-arm-k} and \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite}); ``budget-matched'' in this paper means the mean, never a per-seed guarantee. On printf, all three arms have a small, expensive numerator (14, 6 and 7 correct answers out of 40 attempts costing full-arm money each), which is why the cost-per-correct figures there (Table~\ref{tab:cost-per-correct}) are an order of magnitude higher than the interpreter family and should be read with that denominator in mind, not as a stable per-answer price. Cache reads dominate token volume on every arm-C run: computed by the same script (\texttt{cacheShares}: cache-read tokens divided by input$+$cache-read$+$cache-creation tokens, summed over each cell's 40 runs), arm C's cache-read share is 94.61\% on \texttt{stamp-interpreter}, 94.25\% on \texttt{stamp-2} and 93.64\% on \texttt{bench-printf-format} -- close to, but not identical to, the 98--99\% figure \texttt{docs/review-round-0918.md} reports for build-room seats, which measured a different (non-benchmark) workload and should not be quoted as this paper's own number. Cache reads are billed at roughly a tenth of fresh input price (\texttt{paper/figures.md}), so this high share is why arm C and arm K's dollar cost grows much more slowly than their raw token counts would suggest.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

On the interpreter family, arm A's failures are highly concentrated but not uniform. Every one of the 7 \texttt{stamp-interpreter} arm-A failures in the confirmatory grid (seeds 101--140) answers 101 where 100 is expected, in the same trailing position of the output (a nested-\texttt{DEF} lexical-closure misreading; verified directly against each failing seed's \texttt{workspace/answer.txt}, the same slip as the ten-seed admission pilot, whose five failures all wrote 101 at the same position, token index 32, the \texttt{CALL outer 1} line: \texttt{tasks/stamp-interpreter/ADMISSION.md}; \texttt{tasks/SUITE.json}'s ``output 32'' refers to that index). \texttt{stamp-2}'s 12 arm-A failures are not one shared answer: 11 give ``1027 1008'' for the expected ``3007 3005'' (the same partial-closure slip at two adjacent output positions, tokens 9 and 10, exactly as in the admission pilot: \texttt{tasks/stamp-2/ADMISSION.md}) and 1 gives ``3007 1008'', correct at the first of the two positions and matching the other 11 at the second (\texttt{paper/prereg-arm-k.md}: ``11 one identical wrong answer, 1 another''; confirmed directly against \texttt{workspace/answer.txt}). Because the dominant wrong answer, on both tasks, is a single shared misreading rather than diverse noise, it is rarely the plurality among $k=8$--10 fresh attempts, so arm K's plurality vote recovers the correct answer in most groups. All four of arm K's \texttt{stamp-interpreter} losses have exactly 4 correct votes out of 10, but they are not uniform in cause (\texttt{paper/generated/rq1-family-table.json}'s \texttt{failingGroupVotes}, joining each group's \texttt{selection.votes} to its attempts' own pass/fail flags): seeds 113 and 122 are true plurality losses, 6 votes for the single shared wrong answer against 4 correct; seeds 116 and 120 are \textbf{4--4 ties} between the correct answer and the single largest wrong answer (the remaining 2 votes are idiosyncratic wrong answers that appear only once each), broken to the wrong side by the pre-registered lowest-sub-seed tie-break rule. \texttt{paper/amendments.md}'s ``(4 to 5 of 10 correct)'' is imprecise in a way that also obscures this distinction -- every group has exactly 4 correct votes, but only half of the four losses are outright plurality losses; the other half are ties the selection rule happened to break the wrong way, which is a different failure mode (a coin-flip-like tie-break, not the wrong answer being genuinely more popular) and belongs in the mechanism discussion, not folded into ``the wrong answer was the plurality.'' Arm C's three-seat deliberation reaches the correct answer on every group (80/80 pooled). \textbf{Why is untested}: a plausible account is that a seat reasoning through the scope rule independently, or a challenge that surfaces the disagreement before a vote, breaks the shared misreading -- but no transcript analysis in this paper's evidence base checks this, so it is stated here as a hypothesis, not a measured mechanism. On printf, arm A's wrong \texttt{\%.17g} formatting is the \emph{modal} behaviour among independent attempts (the complement of its 14/40 pass rate, Table~\ref{tab:per-task}); \texttt{paper/amendments.md}'s printf selector calibration reports that, of the 40 arm-A candidates in the confirmatory grid, the 26 of the 40 that fail are signature-identical to each other on all 9650 README-derived probes (as are the 14 that pass, to each other), so those 26 failures are the same underlying case, not merely the same known trap independently. So both an aggregation method that rewards agreement (arm K's MBR-exec, \S\ref{sec:results-arm-k}) and a three-seat deliberation that must also reach agreement to conclude (arm C) do worse than one unaggregated attempt (arm A 14/40 vs.\ arm K 7/40 vs.\ arm C 6/40). Whether arm C's 34 printf failures share the same \texttt{1e-07} case as arm A's is \textbf{not recorded}: \texttt{bench/results/rq1-suite}'s arm-C results carry only an oracle pass/fail exit code, not which hidden case failed, so this paper does not claim they are the same failure, only that both arms fail at a similar rate on the same known single-trap task. The common thread this section can support is that whether the shared failure mode is a minority or a majority position among independent attempts coincides, on these three tasks, with whether aggregation (voting or deliberation) helped or hurt; \emph{why} deliberation specifically tracks that same pattern, rather than only sampling-based voting, is discussed as an open question in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
